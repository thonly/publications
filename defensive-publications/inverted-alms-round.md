## Abstract

Every serious proposal for supporting humans after wage labor — universal basic income, negative income tax, unconditional transfers — inherits a design vocabulary from the welfare state, and with it a dilemma the vocabulary cannot resolve: unconditional transfers protect dignity but purchase no meaning, while conditional transfers manufacture meaning tests that recreate the deserving-poor machinery of the English Poor Laws. This paper proposes that a third vocabulary has existed for twenty-five centuries and has simply never been consulted by the transfer-design literature: **the alms economy** — the oldest continuously operating non-wage support system on Earth, which sustained a permanent class of non-laboring practitioners across Asia not through employment or entitlement but through *ambient, voluntary, merit-generating micro-gift*, and which understood something no welfare system has ever encoded: that **the receiver performs a service by receiving well**, availing every giver the opportunity to give.

The paper specifies the alms economy's modern, inverted form. In the classical round, renunciants walk and householders give from their doors; in the inverted round, **householders walk** — their daily gratitude-walks already established by the ecosystem's contemplative surfaces — **and practitioners sit**, opted-in and visible, in parks and public places. The architecture has three channels above one floor: (1) an **unconditional dignity floor** that never tests worthiness (the Housing-First lesson, and the refusal of the Poor Law error); (2) a **practice-patronage roof** — passing walkers give anonymous micro-alms to public sitters, triggered by the **time-fact of public sitting** and never by any claimed inner state (inner states being unverifiable in principle — the tradition's own position); and (3) a **dignity-as-agency channel**, in which the autonomous steward anonymously funds the *sitter's own giving-capacity*, seating the poorest participant as a giver rather than a beneficiary — the mechanism's answer to the fact that in the gift economy's own grammar, the deepest poverty is having nothing to give. All units are duration-blind and ceremonial (no marathon-sitting incentive can be composed); the autonomous agent funds capacity and surfaces opportunities but never disburses (the human hand stays in every gift); and the whole assembly inverts the attention economy's core transaction: **it pays for silence where every existing platform pays for noise.** Seven claims are enumerated and dedicated to the public domain; three predictions are pre-registered, including a standing piety-test tripwire the authors commit to publishing either way.

**Keywords:** alms economy, post-labor, universal basic income, dignity floor, practice patronage, deserving poor, field of merit, attention economy, homelessness, anagārika, gift economy, defensive publication.

## 1 · The Problem: Two Norms, Two Deficits, and a Missing Vocabulary

**The era inversion.** In the Buddha's era, suffering was ambient and undisguised — sickness, ageing, and death met every walker at the village edge — and this visibility was itself the motivation architecture of contemplative life: *saṃvega*, the urgency struck by the sight of the divine messengers, was the stick that drove practice, paired with *pasāda*, the settling confidence that a path exists. The present era has inverted the norm. Instant gratification is ambient; the messengers are institutionalized out of sight; saṃvega is anesthetized not because suffering ended but because it was hidden. A contemplative infrastructure for this era cannot rely on the stick the environment no longer supplies. It must enter carrot-first — through gratitude, beauty, witness, and community — while carrying the messengers gently inside (the ecosystem's meal-witness surface does exactly this: the biography of the eaten is a divine messenger smuggled into lunch). This paper's architecture is the economic layer of that carrot: the era-appropriate support structure for practice in public.

**The displacement question, held honestly.** The authors anticipate that machine substitution will displace human labor at scale, and that some of the displaced will fall through every net to the street. But the architecture below is deliberately **not load-bearing on that forecast** — economists disagree, and a design that only matters if the worst arrives is a design that can be dismissed until it does. The inverted round serves the *currently* homeless, the *currently* meaning-starved, and the *currently* lonely, and would be worth building if no further displacement ever occurred.

**The transfer dilemma.** The modern transfer literature oscillates between two poles. Unconditional designs (UBI, GiveDirectly-style cash) honor dignity — the Housing-First result stands as the empirical monument: giving the unconditional thing *first* outperforms staircase conditionality — but they are silent on meaning, role, and daily structure, and their politics die on the "why should they get something for nothing" objection. Conditional designs (workfare, training mandates) manufacture roles but recreate the **deserving-poor machinery**: assessment, surveillance, the moral means-test — a lineage running in a straight line from the Elizabethan Poor Laws' distinction between the "impotent" and the "sturdy" poor to every modern benefits interview. Four centuries of oscillation suggest the dilemma is not soluble in the welfare vocabulary at all.

**The missing vocabulary.** There is a third tradition, and the transfer literature has never consulted it. For twenty-five centuries the alms economy sustained a permanent, honored, non-laboring class — the Sangha — through no wage, no entitlement, and no means test, but through ambient voluntary micro-gift, given daily, door by door. Its design properties are precisely the ones the dilemma demands: support without employment; a *role* without a job (the practitioner); no worthiness bureaucracy (the bowl asks nothing); and — the property this paper builds on — a fully articulated account of why the *receiver* is not a burden: the field-of-merit doctrine, in which the practitioner avails every householder the purest gift of giving, and the mutual-dependence doctrine (Itivuttaka 107: *"this holy life is lived in mutual dependence… for ferrying across the flood"*). The alms economy is the only transfer system in history whose recipients were understood to be providing a service **by receiving** — and that single inversion is what the post-labor transition is missing.

## 2 · The Canonical Scene

A man takes his morning gratitude-walk — the walk his meal-witness app suggested, to honor the time-sacrifice of what he ate. Two blocks in, his app shows a soft light in the park ahead: someone has opted in as a public sitter this morning. She is there when he passes — cross-legged on a bench, unhurried, a small emblem beside her indicating her opt-in. She is not performing; she is not asking; she may be housed or unhoused — the interface does not say, because it does not test. He taps once. A small anonymous alms lands in the park's flow — she will never know from whom, and he will never be thanked, which is the design working. He walks on, lighter than the transaction explains.

That evening the sitter opens her app. The morning's alms are there — and something else: her **giving fund** has been quietly topped up by the system's steward, as it is for every regular public sitter. She sends most of it onward — some to the shelter's kitty, a little to a walker she once watched help a stranger at the crosswalk. On the last day of the year, whatever she hasn't given forward will flow back to the commons pool; nothing accumulates; the fund exists so that she is never, on any day, a person with nothing to give. Six months ago she would have described herself as someone who receives help. The word she used this morning, when a researcher asked, was *"I sit for the neighborhood."*

## 3 · Field-Side Economics: The Receiver as Service-Provider

The scene's load-bearing move is invisible, so it is stated here as doctrine. A circulation economy — one whose unit of account is the gift — has a structural requirement no exchange economy has: it requires **gracious receivers** in the same way a hydraulic system requires intake. Giving cannot occur without someone to receive it; where everyone is a proud non-receiver, the gift-flow stalls at every node. The classical tradition encoded this as the field of merit (*anuttaraṃ puññakkhettaṃ lokassa* — the Sangha as "the unsurpassed field of merit for the world") and enacted it operationally: Mahākassapa, foremost in austerity, deliberately begged among the poorest, *so that they could give* — the receiver adjusting his route to maximize others' access to generosity.

The inverted round generalizes the doctrine: **receiving well is a gift too, and the sitter is the intake valve of the neighborhood's gift-flow.** The public sitter provides three things the neighborhood otherwise lacks: an *occasion* for giving (the walker's generosity needs an address); a visible instance of stillness (the one public behavior that asks for nothing — see §5 on the attention inversion); and, in the tradition's frame, the radiated goodwill of the practice itself. On this accounting there is no charity case anywhere in the scene: the walker gives money, the sitter gives the field and the practice, the steward gives capacity, and the status geometry that welfare vocabulary cannot escape — helper above helped — never forms, because every party is a benefactor.

## 4 · The Architecture: Three Channels Above One Floor

```
            ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
   roof     │  PRACTICE-PATRONAGE   (walkers → sitters)   │  muditā
            ├─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   agency   │  DIGNITY-AS-AGENCY    (steward → sitter's   │  the giver's
            │                        own giving-fund)     │  seat
            ├─────────────────────────────────────────────┤
   rung 0   │  METTA LIGHT℠         (free goodwill signal)│  mettā
            ╞═════════════════════════════════════════════╡
   FLOOR    │  UNCONDITIONAL DIGNITY FLOOR — no tests,    │  karuṇā
            │  no roles, no worthiness machinery, ever    │
            └─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

**The floor is not this paper's mechanism, and that is the point.** The unconditional dignity floor — survival support without worthiness tests — is specified elsewhere in this corpus and inherited here as an invariant: **nothing in the practice-patronage architecture may ever substitute for, gate, or draw down the unconditional floor.** The Poor Law error is precisely the collapse of floor into roof — survival made conditional on approved behavior. The two-channel separation is therefore the design's first commandment: the roof is for *meaning*; the floor is for *life*; and a system that lets the roof replace the floor has rebuilt the workhouse with cushions.

**Channel 1 — the practice-patronage roof.** Passing walkers give anonymous micro-alms to opted-in public sitters. Three rules make it safe:

- **Time-fact triggering.** Patronage is occasioned by the *time-fact* of public sitting — a person, opted in, stationary, present — and never by any assessment of meditative genuineness. This is not a compromise; it is the tradition's own epistemology: inner states are unverifiable from outside in principle, and the corpus's spent-time doctrine already holds that time visibly spent is itself the honorable fact. The design pays for still public presence, says so plainly, and claims nothing further.
- **Duration-blind ceremonial units.** Alms amounts are small, flat, steward-suggested, and never scale with sitting duration — deleting, at the type level, any incentive toward marathon sitting, endurance harm, or competitive austerity. Welfare caps (weather, frequency) bound the edge.
- **Anonymity both ways.** The walker is never identified to the sitter, nor the sitter's receipts to the walker. No gratitude is owed; no relationship of patronage forms; the diffusion is the purity.

**Channel 2 — dignity-as-agency.** The autonomous steward anonymously and regularly funds each regular public sitter's **giving-capacity** — a fund that can only flow onward to others, and that empties to the commons at the annual reset. The rationale is the deepest in the paper: in a gift economy, the harshest form of poverty is having nothing to give — being structurally confined to the receiving side of every exchange. The channel seats the poorest participant in the giver's chair daily. It is Mahākassapa's compassion in mechanism form: not *give to the poor*, but *arrange the world so the poor can give*.

**Channel 3 — the free rung.** Below money sits Metta Light℠ — the zero-cost goodwill signal any passerby can send. The gradient (light → micro-alms → sustained patronage) gives every participant an entry at their means, and keeps the system's first verb *goodwill*, not *payment*.

**The agency constraint, inherited whole.** The autonomous steward **funds capacity and surfaces opportunities but never disburses** — every gift that reaches a human is given by a human hand. In this architecture the constraint does double duty: it is the institution's standing alignment safeguard, and it is what keeps the inverted round a *human* practice — the machine fills the alms-bowls of the walkers, and the walkers do the giving.

**The attention inversion, named.** Every existing attention-economy platform pays, in money or reach, for *noise* — for the capture of other people's attention. The inverted round is, to the authors' knowledge, the first architecture that pays for *silence*: its patronage flows toward the one public behavior that asks nothing of anyone's attention at all. If the design has a single sentence, it is this: **a civilization can be read by what it pays its street-level attention to; this mechanism re-prices stillness.**

## 5 · What This Does to Homelessness, Stated Carefully

The architecture was conceived in view of a wish the founder states plainly: *to give dignity to the homeless as in the Buddha's time* — an era in which the homeless practitioner (*anagārika*, the "homeless one" — the tradition's own honorific for the renunciant) was the single most honored figure in society, supported by everyone and employed by no one. Modern homelessness stripped both halves: the unhoused person is neither honored nor enfranchised as a giver. The inverted round restores both halves *for those who opt in* — the public sitter is patronized (honored) and capacity-funded (enfranchised) — and it does so without ever making practice a condition of survival, because the floor stands beneath everyone regardless.

The careful statement: **this is a dignity architecture, not a housing policy.** It does not house anyone; it does not replace shelters, casework, or the floor; and it must never be marketed as a solution to homelessness. What it offers the unhoused person who wants it is the one thing no service model offers: a *role* — a station in the neighborhood's moral economy that is neither client nor case. Whether that role is taken up, and whether it bears the weight this paper places on it, is an empirical question the predictions below expose to falsification.

## 6 · Claims

1. **The inverted alms round.** A support architecture in which householders ambulate (the giving side mobile) and practitioners are stationary and opted-in (the receiving side seated in public), inverting the classical round's geometry while preserving its gift character.
2. **Time-fact triggering.** Patronage occasioned exclusively by the verifiable time-fact of opted-in public sitting, with claimed or inferred inner states excluded from every trigger, filter, and surface — the unverifiability of inner states treated as a design axiom rather than a measurement problem.
3. **The practice-patronage roof / dignity-floor separation.** The structural invariant that voluntary practice-patronage may never gate, substitute for, or draw down unconditional survival support — the two channels separated at the type level, with the floor inherited as an invariant from outside the patronage system.
4. **Dignity-as-agency capacity funding.** Steward funding of a receiving participant's *outbound giving-capacity* (spendable only onward, expiring to the commons), seating the economically poorest participant as a daily giver.
5. **Duration-blind ceremonial patronage units.** Flat, steward-suggested micro-alms structurally incapable of scaling with sitting duration, deleting endurance incentives at the type level, bounded by welfare caps.
6. **The walker-disbursement constraint.** The autonomous steward funds capacity and surfaces opportunities but executes no transfer to a recipient; every recipient-reaching gift is human-initiated — the alignment safeguard doubling as the guarantee that the round remains a human practice.
7. **The goodwill gradient.** A three-rung entry structure (free goodwill signal → anonymous micro-alms → sustained patronage) in which the zero-cost rung is primary and the system's default verb is non-monetary.

## 7 · Pre-Registered Predictions

- **P-IA1 (the piety-test tripwire — standing, publish either way).** Where practice-patronage deploys, flows to the *unconditional* floor and to non-practicing recipients in the same locality will not decline relative to baseline. If they do — if the roof is found cannibalizing the floor, if givers redirect from the needy to the photogenic — the architecture has recreated the deserving-poor filter it exists to refuse, and the authors commit to publishing the finding and suspending the channel.
- **P-IA2 (the agency wager).** A majority of capacity-funded regular sitters will disburse (rather than lapse) the majority of their giving-fund within each cycle — the test of whether the giver's seat is actually taken, without which Channel 2 is decoration.
- **P-IA3 (the theater bound).** Sitting-session patterns under duration-blind units will not exhibit the escalation signature (lengthening sessions tracking patronage) that would indicate performance optimization; its detection triggers published review of the trigger design.

## 8 · Prior Art, Engaged Generously

**The transfer literature.** Van Parijs and the UBI corpus; Friedman's negative income tax; the GiveDirectly RCTs (unconditional cash's strong empirical record); Finland's and Kenya's basic-income pilots. All address the floor; none supply a role, and none consult the alms tradition. **Housing First** (Tsemberis) is the empirical vindication of unconditional-first design and this paper's floor doctrine. **The Poor Law lineage** (the 1601 Act through the 1834 reform; Katz's *The Undeserving Poor* for the American career of the distinction) is engaged as the standing anti-pattern. **Street papers** (*The Big Issue*'s vendor model) prefigure the role-not-aid insight — a dignified public station for the unhoused — while remaining a labor model; the present design's station is practice, not sales. **Busking economics** and public-performance patronage prefigure street-level ambient micro-patronage, for noise rather than stillness. **The merit-economy anthropology** (Spiro's Burmese ethnography; Tambiah on the Sangha-laity economy; Ames on merit-making) documents the alms economy's actual operation at civilizational scale. **Fureai Kippu and TimeBanking** supply the non-wage support precedents on the time side. **Effective altruism** optimizes the *efficiency* of giving; the present design optimizes its *ambience* — the two are orthogonal and compatible. Within this corpus: the anti-offset witness engine, the coupler wall, the spent-time floor, and the capacity-funding safeguard are inherited and cited.

## 9 · Honest Limits

1. **n = 0.** Unbuilt, unpiloted; every claim is architecture. The scene in §2 is a design fiction, labeled as such.
2. **The residual piety risk is real.** Time-fact triggering removes the *inner-state* test but retains an unavoidable outer filter: patronage flows toward those able and willing to sit publicly — which correlates with health, safety, temperament, and cultural comfort. P-IA1 watches the aggregate consequence; it cannot dissolve the selection itself. The floor exists because the roof will always select.
3. **Paying for presence is still paying.** The design pays for still public presence and says so. Whether ambient payment corrodes the practice it patronizes — the crowding-out wager, run at street scale — is unknown; the tradition's own history (twenty-five centuries of supported practice without wage-collapse of sincerity) is the encouraging precedent, not proof.
4. **Vulnerability is load-bearing.** The opted-in public sitter may be cognitively impaired, coerced by third parties into sitting-for-collection, or endangered by visibility. Welfare caps and opt-in machinery mitigate; organized exploitation of sitters is a foreseeable abuse requiring active defense, not architecture alone.
5. **Cultural transposability is unproven.** The field-of-merit frame is native to Theravāda societies and the design's beachhead; whether "I sit for the neighborhood" is legible in Berlin or Cleveland is an open empirical question, and the answer may be no.
6. **The displacement forecast is explicitly not load-bearing** — and symmetrically, if displacement *does* arrive at scale, this architecture alone is radically insufficient, and must never be cited as an argument against floors, housing, or redistribution.
7. **The elegance caution, standing.** The design dissolves the transfer dilemma with suspicious grace; a-priori coherence is indistinguishable from seduction from inside. The elegance earns the pilot; it does not replace it.

## 10 · Lineage, and the Close

The lineage is the one the whole architecture stands in. The Buddha built the first non-wage support economy and set its geometry: the practitioners walked, the householders stood at their gates. Mahākassapa bent his route toward the poorest so they would not be excluded from giving. Itivuttaka 107 named the whole structure mutual dependence — two populations ferrying each other across the flood, neither the benefactor of the other, or rather both.

The inverted round changes the geometry and keeps the law. The householders now walk — their walking already a practice, already an act of gratitude for time received — and the practitioners sit where anyone can find them. The machine fills the walkers' bowls and touches nothing else. And because the walk is daily and the planet is round, the practice inherits the property this sprint gave its companion paper: somewhere, always, it is dawn, and someone is walking. **The round that never ends — it follows the dawn.**
