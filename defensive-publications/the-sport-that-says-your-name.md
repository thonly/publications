## Abstract

Every sport in history was designed under three scarcities. **Umpires are scarce**, so games tolerate bad calls, and the figure of the referee absorbs abuse so chronic it is now collapsing youth-sports officiating in multiple countries. **Fair opponents are scarce**, so sport is organized as segregation infrastructure — by age, sex, size, and skill — and the one social unit that most needs shared play, the mixed-age family, is precisely the unit no sport accommodates. **Witness is scarce**, so games keep score of *persons*, and the counting hardens into statistics, rankings, and fame hierarchies — the attention economy's atom, present at the playground. Machine intelligence ends all three scarcities at once. A sport *designed for* that abundance — rather than an old sport with cameras added — can spend it on what the scarcities stole: inclusion, giving, and story.

This paper specifies such a sport. **B-Sey™** is a circle game descended from the Southeast Asian *sey* (Khmer សី, the dusk game of collective keep-alive), played by one to N players with foot or paddle, with or without a net, cooperatively or adversarially — and organized by one mechanism with no precedent in sport: **the caller**. An AI intermediary announces, before each strike, the *name* of the player who must receive next — distributing touches **equally yet randomly**, so that participation is egalitarian, attention is universal (anyone's name may be next), and, as a structural by-product, *strangers learn each other's names through play*. Because the caller designates receivers on both sides of the net, targeting a weaker opponent is not merely discouraged but **absent from the game's move-set** — to the authors' knowledge the first net sport in which exploiting the weakest human present is structurally impossible. The point is **dyadic** — it increments on successful *receipt*, so every score is a completed give-and-receive and no one scores alone. Handicaps are applied **at the start line, never in the stream**: unequal teams begin at unequal countdown numbers on a coarse fixed lattice (multiples of three), a quantization that makes the handicap read as a garment size rather than a skill rating. Cooperative play counts *up* (the collective streak); adversarial play counts *down* to zero — first to nothing wins. The announcement reaches the circle in one of three named formats, of which the default — **the relay call** — speaks the name *privately to a single rotating human*, who calls it aloud: fairness stays machine-held while delivery is human, so the sport runs on **one borrowed device** with no loudspeaker, no network, and no clock synchronization, and the rotating relay is where participants practise *producing* the names they otherwise only hear. Where devices are plentiful, a **synced-device mesh** speaks the call in unison instead, and may render score and celebration in each phone's own language.

The sport is parameterized on three orthogonal axes — **method** (the body vocabulary each player declares: hand, paddle, or foot, mixed freely within one circle), **mode** (what the circle plays against: nothing, its own record, or another circle), and **level** (the *lead time* between the call and the object's arrival). The default state has **no score at all**, and its stated purpose is that everyone learns everyone's name with correct pronunciation; at the longest lead time the called player raises a hand, so the ladder from easy to hard is a **scaffold-fading memory curve** rather than a skill ladder — the game becomes more difficult exactly as the group's name recall improves. Scoring degrades gracefully across three tiers — **implied by the call**, **validated by a k-of-N vote of the circle's own microphones**, or **confirmed by an accelerometer in the object** — none of which gates play, and all of which are licensed to be loose because the score moves no value. The system is **local-first**: the entire game runs on one device, offline, with no accounts. **Sixteen claims** are enumerated and dedicated to the public domain; **five predictions** are pre-registered, including the design's own existential wager, which the authors commit to publishing either way.

**Keywords:** designed sport, AI officiating, circle game, sey, chinlone, sepak takraw, mixed-age play, handicap systems, loneliness intervention, third place, name learning, cooperative games, defensive publication.

## 1 · The Problem: What Sport Optimized For, and What It Cost

Sport is one of humanity's great social technologies, and like every technology it embodies the constraints of its era. Three of those constraints did most of the shaping.

**The officiating constraint.** A fair game needs a neutral judge, and neutral judges are expensive. Most human play therefore proceeds unofficiated — with the familiar result that disputes end games, dominant personalities adjudicate in their own favor, and children learn early that the loudest voice owns the rules. Where officiating exists, it concentrates in a single fallible human who becomes the game's lightning rod: abuse of referees is now so severe that officiating associations in the United States, the United Kingdom, and elsewhere report recruitment collapse, with youth leagues canceled for want of anyone willing to be shouted at. The referee crisis is not a culture problem that better manners will fix; it is a *structure* problem — one human absorbing the full adversarial pressure of everyone else's stakes.

**The matching constraint.** A good game needs opponents of comparable ability, and comparable opponents are rare. Sport's solution was segregation: leagues by age, divisions by skill, categories by sex and weight. The solution works — and quietly forecloses the most valuable game that could exist: the one where a grandmother, a teenager, and a six-year-old contest the same point and all three matter. The family, the one social unit whose play would repair the most, is the unit sport's architecture serves worst. (The lone mainstream counterexample, golf's handicap system, proves the demand: it is the reason golf is one of very few sports a parent and child can genuinely contest, and it required a century of institutional machinery to maintain.)

**The witness constraint.** A game's story needs recording, and recording was expensive, so sport recorded the cheapest summary: *who scored*. From that compression grew the entire statistical culture of sport — batting averages, rankings, ladders, most-valuable-player awards — a fame hierarchy erected on a bookkeeping shortcut. The consequences run downhill to the playground: the child picked last exists because informal sport, too, keeps score of persons; the pickup game has a status economy, and everyone present knows their rank in it.

Machine intelligence ends all three scarcities. Officiating becomes abundant (a phone judges a line call better than a linesman); matching becomes computable (fairness can be *manufactured* rather than found); witness becomes total (everything can be recorded, which forces the question of what *should* be). The default path — already visibly underway in the patent literature (§10) — is to spend this abundance on the old game: more statistics, finer player-surveillance, engagement-optimized training loops. The sport specified below spends it in the opposite direction: on inclusion the matching constraint forbade, on giving the scoring compression erased, and on the one output no sport has ever produced on purpose — *strangers who know each other's names*.

A design axiom governs throughout, shared by all three papers of this sprint and coined here as **the play/currency wall**: *competition and play may generate the story; only witnessed giving moves the currency.* Winning, in any game this institution builds, may move narrative, honor, or the address of an already-committed gift — never value itself. The wall's application to stakes and patronage is its own paper (*The Wager That Isn't*, this sprint); its application here is simpler and prior: in B-Sey, there is nothing to move. What victory produces is a story.

## 2 · The Canonical Scenes

**A park, late afternoon.** A man finishes a quiet quarter-hour on a bench — the contemplative practice is its own product and no part of this paper — and walks toward a loose ring of people batting a rattan-weight ball into the air. He does not know any of them. Someone widens the circle; he types a name into the app — just *Virak* — and his phone joins the mesh. A moment later every phone in the circle says, in one voice: *"Sey says: Virak!"* The ball comes to him — gently; the woman who sent it saw a newcomer, and delivery is the whole skill of this game. He gets his paddle under it. The circle cheers the streak alive. Within three rounds, everyone in the circle has heard his name four times, and he has heard all of theirs, because the game cannot proceed without saying them. When the streak dies at 41 — nine short of nothing in particular, since the next rite waits at 333 — the circle groans and laughs, and a stranger says, "Again, Virak?" That sentence — a stranger using his name to invite him back — is the sport's actual product. The rally was the packaging.

**A living room, that evening.** Three generations, one net strung between two chairs. The eight-year-old and her grandmother hold paddles; the parents play with their feet. The family's phones announce in two languages — the grandmother's in Khmer, the child's in English — one circle, one game, two tongues. The teams are uneven, so the app has set the start numbers: the parents begin at 33, the paddle side at 21. First to zero. Nobody chose those numbers; nobody can argue with them; and because the caller decides who receives, the parents cannot serve at the eight-year-old to win — the game has no such move. When the paddle side takes its last point — actively won, as the final point must be — the child performs a victory lap of the sofa. What she has won is the story of having beaten her parents fairly. That is all winning moves here, and it is enough; it has always been enough.

**A classroom, the first week of school.** Thirty children who mostly do not know each other, one teacher who needs to learn thirty names, one ball. The circle solves both problems in a single period, because equal-yet-random guarantees every child is called — the game is structurally incapable of ignoring anyone — and the teacher, standing inside the circle, hears every name spoken aloud dozens of times. No child is picked last, because there is no picking. No child dominates, because the caller will not allow the ball to orbit the confident. The physical-education period and the social-integration project turn out to be the same forty minutes.

## 3 · The Design: One Circle, Five Seats

B-Sey is a circle game for **one to N players**, played with a ball designed to be struck by **foot or paddle** — the athletic adult kicks, the grandparent and the child paddle, and both choices are ordinary equipment decisions, not accommodations. Play proceeds in two modes. **Without a net**, the circle plays cooperative keep-alive: the score counts *up*, one per completed reception, and the object is the longest unbroken streak — the collective form. **With a net**, two sides contest a countdown (§5–6) under volleyball-like rally structure — the adversarial form, and the form on which the companion paper's challenge grammar (B-Match℠) nests. A newcomer joins either form the same way: the circle widens. There are no teams to pick in circle mode and no tryout in either; the topology itself is the invitation.

The machine intelligence in the sport occupies exactly **four seats** — including the one the sport is named for — and is barred from the fifth. The seats fall into two classes, and the division is not a taxonomy for its own sake: it decides how each seat's output can be *checked*, and therefore what an implementer is permitted to put in it.

**The determined seats.** Their output is a pure function of inputs declared before play, so it is checkable by **recomputation** — anyone holding the inputs reproduces the result exactly and sees for themselves that the rule was honoured.

1. **Caller.** The sport's defining mechanism (§4): before each play, the name of the required next receiver, announced aloud. Its selection policy is equal-yet-random and, given the circle and a declared seed, *deterministic* (§4.2) — an arithmetic distribution, not a live opinion about anybody.
2. **Equalizer.** Fairness is manufactured at two points and only two: the **start line** (asymmetric countdown numbers, §6) and the **implement** (paddle or foot, chosen by the player), both drawn from a coarse published lattice. It is never manufactured mid-play. The distinction is load-bearing and stated as a hard rule below.

**The judged seats.** Their output is a perception or a narration. It cannot be reproduced from inputs, so it is checkable only by **explanation** — each judgment stating its ground in one sentence, the standard §5 already imposes on the last-gift ruling.

3. **Umpire.** Line calls, receipt validity, and the classification of the final point (§5) are the system's judgments — instant, neutral, and not attached to a human being who can be shouted at. The referee crisis is answered not by braver referees but by removing the seat the abuse was aimed at.
4. **Witness.** The system records the game's story — the streak, the rally, the celebration — as a *collective* chronicle. It records no player statistics: no individual completion rates, no rankings, no most-valuable anything (§7–8). What it counts, it counts about the circle.

**The barred seat.**

5. **Player — never.** No machine plays. In an age when most activities can be delegated to software, the sport is constructed entirely from the one thing that cannot be: bodies, co-present, attending to each other. The machine is present so that the humans can be.

**The seat-migration bar.** A seat may not take on the other class's mode of operation, and the prohibition runs in both directions because both failures arrive at the same place — a machine-held ranking of persons. A *determined* seat that acquires judgment becomes a rating engine: a caller that routes around a struggling receiver, or a handicapper that tunes start numbers to measured ability, has evaluated the players, and has done it where no one can see it. A *judged* seat that acquires distribution authority becomes a caller: an umpire empowered to influence who receives next can express a preference about people through a channel built for judgments about the ball. The rule is short enough to hold in mind while implementing: **the determined seats never judge a person; the judged seats never choose a person.**

### 3.1 · The three axes: method, mode, level

The sport is parameterized on three orthogonal axes. They are not settings; they are a **ladder**, and an implementation that surfaces them as a configuration screen has already failed the design.

```
   AXIS      QUESTION IT ANSWERS         VALUES
   ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   METHOD    what part of you gives?     hand · paddle · foot
   MODE      what do you play against?   nothing · your record · another circle
   LEVEL     how much warning?           long lead · moderate · short lead
```

**Method** is the body vocabulary a player declares — hand-catch-and-release, paddle, or foot. Anatomy plus implement exhausts the list; there is no fourth. The values are **defaults suggested by ability, never assignments by category**, and heterogeneous methods are expected *within a single circle*: a grandparent with a paddle, a child with hands, an adult with feet, one shared object. This is the load-bearing case, not the exotic one — a sport that segregates by method has thrown away the mixed-age property that is the design's principal social claim, and would have reintroduced by the back door the segregation §1 identifies as sport's inherited disease.

A declared method **is a start-line handicap** (§6) — public, self-chosen, announced before play, never assigned during it — so the axis requires no new fairness machinery; it inherits the machinery already specified. Two consequences follow:

- **The object is constrained by the axis.** One shared object must be catchable, kickable, and paddle-able. A hard ball fails hands; a shuttlecock fails paddle power. A **soft weighted sack** satisfies all three, which is why the reference implementation is a sack rather than a ball.
- **A catch stops flight, so possession needs a bound.** Hand players receive by catching, which would otherwise let a player hold the object and stall the circle. The rule: **catch and release within one beat, where the caller's own cadence is the release clock.** As lead time shortens (§4.1) the hand method converts from catch-and-throw to volley-tap without a rule change — difficulty emerges from the method mixture rather than being imposed on it.

**Mode** is what the circle plays against, and the values ascend: *nothing* (**Friendship**, no scoring of any kind, three or more players), *its own record* (**Streak**, no net, ascending collective count, one or more players — a circle of one is still a circle), *another circle* (**Team**, net, descending count to zero, two or more players). The mode names are deliberate: **no label on the product surface names rivalry**, though the adversarial mode is genuinely adversarial and this paper describes it as such. Naming the third mode for its structural novelty — *sides exist* — rather than for its disposition is a small instance of a general discipline in this corpus, in which vocabulary is held to the same standard as mechanism.

The mode ladder is also the **adoption funnel**, and an unusually honest one: each rung is entered by the circle's own election, each rung's graduation is the previous rung's success condition, and the entry rung has **no score at all**.

**Level** is treated in §4.1, because it is a property of the caller rather than of the players.

**The three-value pattern is not aesthetic and should not be read as one.** Each axis has an independent reason for its cardinality: method is exhausted by anatomy plus implement; mode is exhausted by the opponent relation; level is coarsely quantized for the same anti-rating reason the start-line lattice is (§6) — a continuously variable difficulty dial would read as a precision skill rating, which is the artifact this design exists to avoid. Where a fourth value would genuinely serve — very large circles may want a fourth lead-time band — the pattern must yield. **Three is a finding here, not a constraint.**

## 4 · The Caller: Equal-Yet-Random, and the Property It Buys

The sport's one genuinely novel mechanism is small enough to state in a sentence: **before each strike, the system announces by name the player who must receive next.**

Everything distinctive about B-Sey follows from that sentence.

**Equal-yet-random distribution.** The caller's selection policy has two commitments that are individually trivial and jointly unprecedented in sport. *Equal*: across a session, every player in the circle receives the same number of touches, within rounding — the weakest player is guaranteed the strongest player's share of the game. *Random*: the order is unpredictable, so no player can disengage — anyone's name may be next, and the suspense that in ordinary sport attaches only to the ball here attaches to every person simultaneously. Equality without randomness would be a queue (predictable, boring, and stigmatizing — everyone can see whose turn is being "given"); randomness without equality is every pickup game ever played (the ball orbits the skilled). The conjunction is the mechanism.

**The cannot-target-weakness property.** In net mode, the caller designates receivers on *both* sides. The attacking side therefore cannot choose to serve at the weakest opponent — the oldest strategy in net sports, taught openly at every level, and the precise mechanism by which the weakest player on any volleyball court spends the match being hunted. In B-Sey that strategy does not exist. It is not penalized; it is not discouraged; it is *absent from the move-set*, the way castling into check is absent from chess. Skill, denied the target-selection channel, migrates entirely into **delivery** — placing the ball so your designated receiver, whoever the caller named, can continue the play. The best player in a B-Sey circle is definitionally the one who makes everyone else look good, because making the named receiver succeed is the only thing the rules allow skill to do. To the authors' knowledge, no prior net sport has this property, and it is claimed here as the design's sharpest single contribution (Claim 2).

**The name as by-product and as point.** Because the call is a *name*, spoken aloud, repeatedly, by every device in the circle (§7), the sport performs introductions as a side effect of its basic loop. This is not a social feature bolted onto a game; it is the game's addressing layer, doing double duty. The design intends the double duty (§9's first prediction measures it): B-Sey is built to be the first sport whose standard output includes *strangers who know each other's names* — the missing primitive of the third place.

**Hard constraints on the caller** (stated here as design law; their governance form lives in the institution's internal directive registry):

- The caller **never evaluates players in the flow of play**. It does not skew calls by skill, win probability, or match situation; it does not route around a struggling receiver at a tense moment. A caller that "protects" the weak player at match point has rebuilt picked-last-at-recess inside an algorithm, invisibly, which is worse than the playground original because it cannot be seen and argued with. Fairness lives at the start line only.
- The caller speaks **chosen names only** — the name a player registers for the circle, which may be a nickname; children's circles use first names or nicknames as a rule. No legal name is ever broadcast across a public park.
- Per-player data that the equalizer uses (implement choice, start-number calibration) is a **private mirror** — visible to the player, never to the circle, never to any ranking, never retained as a skill profile.

### 4.1 · Level as call lead time: a scaffold-fading memory curve

The naive difficulty dial for a called sport is the interval between calls. It is the wrong variable: the inter-call interval is confounded by circle size, so the same setting produces a frantic game with four players and a languid one with thirty. The correct variable is **call lead time** — the interval between the naming of the receiver and the object's arrival. Lead time is invariant to circle size and to method, and it is what a receiver actually experiences as pressure.

Three bands are specified, and what changes across them is not merely speed but **how much external scaffolding supports the act of recognition**:

```
   LEVEL     LEAD TIME    SCAFFOLD                    WHAT IS BEING TRAINED
   ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   Easy      long         called player RAISES A HAND  nothing yet — the
                          and may speak their own      circle is being taught
                          name aloud                   the names
   Medium    moderate     none — the giver must find   name → face recall
                          the named face unaided
   Hard      short        none; the call may land      automaticity under
                          while the object is aloft    time pressure
```

At the longest lead the called player **raises a hand**, which removes the recall requirement entirely: a giver who does not yet know who *Sokha* is can simply deliver to the raised hand. The scaffold is then withdrawn — first the hand, then the time — and the withdrawal is enforced by physics rather than by rule, since a hand cannot usefully be raised inside a short lead.

The result is that **the difficulty ladder is a memory curve, not a skill curve.** A circle can only ascend it as its members' name recall becomes fast and then automatic, which yields the design's cleanest coincidence: *the game becomes more fun exactly as everyone learns each other's names.* The thing the players want and the thing the sport is for are the same thing, and no exhortation is required to align them.

It also supplies the sport's non-numeric progression. In Friendship mode there is no score, and the circle nonetheless has something that advances: **tempo is the score that is not a score.**

**A constraint the implementer must not violate.** It is tempting to advance the level automatically from measured response latency. Doing so *per player* would reconstruct, invisibly and in the tempo dimension, precisely the artifact §4 exists to prevent — a machine-maintained ranking of who is slow. The rule:

> **The level is a property of the circle, never of a player.**

Permitted implementations, in order of preference: (a) **the circle elects the level**, with the caller permitted to *offer* an advance; (b) if adaptive at all, adaptation conditions **only on circle-aggregate state**, ephemeral and on-device, never per-player, never stored, never displayed. Never permitted: a per-player recall score, in any form, visible or not.

### 4.2 · The determined caller: fairness as arithmetic

The caller is the seat where an implementer will most want to put intelligence, and the seat where intelligence does the most damage. This design resolves the tension by removing the caller from the domain of judgment altogether: **the call sequence is a deterministic pure function of the circle's roster and a seed declared before play.** Given the same roster and seed, every conforming implementation emits a bit-identical sequence, which makes the generator a cross-platform contract rather than an internal detail.

Three properties follow. The third was not anticipated when the constraint was adopted, and is now the reason to keep it.

**Fairness becomes verifiable rather than trusted.** Equal-yet-random (§4) is the sport's central promise and the one a participant is least able to check by watching, since over any short window a fair sequence and a biased one look alike — which is precisely why a sport that merely *asserts* fair distribution asks to be taken at its word. When the roster and seed are the entire input, any participant, parent, teacher, or competitor can recompute a completed session and confirm that the distribution was equal and that no one was skipped, favoured, or quietly protected. Publishing the generator is therefore part of the mechanism rather than a disclosure concession.

**Multi-device play stops requiring a network.** Because each device computes the sequence independently, devices in one circle need never be told *who* is next — only **how many calls have elapsed**. A single integer, not a name, is the whole synchronization payload; a device rejoining after a lapse fast-forwards to the current index instead of recovering state. Where simultaneous announcement (§7) demands tight clock alignment, sequence *agreement* demands none at all. The offline-first commitment of §7.1 is thus not merely compatible with the determined caller — it is largely delivered by it.

**There is no per-player state to leak, subpoena, or repurpose.** The forbidden artifact of §4 and §4.1 — a machine-maintained record of who is slow, who is skipped, who is protected — is not merely prohibited here by policy. A pure function of a roster and a seed has nowhere to keep such a record. The guarantee moves out of the governance layer and into the arithmetic, where a later release cannot quietly relax it and an acquirer cannot monetize it.

**The general lesson, offered to implementers of any called game.** A fairness property enforced by a rule *about* the algorithm lasts exactly as long as the organization enforcing it; the same property enforced by determinism and published vectors outlasts the organization. Where a guarantee can be made checkable, making it checkable is worth more than making the underlying decision cleverer — and the two are usually opposed, because the intelligence that would improve a decision is the same thing that makes it unauditable.

None of this disarms the machine; it relocates it. Perception and narration — was the ball in, was the final point actively won, what is this circle's story — are irreducibly judgments, and they are where sensing and inference legitimately belong. The boundary is worth restating at the hardware layer beside the warning already given there (§7.1): **instrumentation serves the judged seats only.** An accelerometer reporting a touch supplies the umpire with evidence. It must never supply the caller with a reason.

## 5 · Scoring: The Dyadic Point and the Two Arrows

**The dyadic scoring unit.** A point increments when the *named receiver successfully receives*. The unit of scoring is therefore not a strike, a kill, or a goal — acts one player performs — but a completed **give-and-receive**: a delivery good enough to be received, and a reception good enough to continue. Nobody scores alone, structurally. The compression error of historical scorekeeping (§1) — recording the last touch and calling it the achievement — is not corrected by tracking assists (a patch several sports have tried) but by defining the point as the *pair*. In the institutional grammar this corpus builds elsewhere, the point is a witnessed micro-gift; within this paper it is enough that it is a *dyad*, and that the sport's atomic event requires two people succeeding together.

**Count up (the circle).** Cooperative keep-alive counts consecutive completed receptions. The streak is a built thing — collective, cumulative, mortal. When it dies, it dies for everyone at once, and the circle starts again: the game's only loss condition is shared by all players equally, which is why circle-mode B-Sey has no losers at all, only a streak that was and the next one. Streak milestones are celebrated at the **repdigits** — 3, 33, 333, 3333 — an exponential rationing that keeps each rite roughly ten times rarer than the last (a celebration every 33 would inflate to wallpaper across a long streak; a celebration at repdigits stays an event for the rest of the sport's life). Between rites, the circle's *record* — the park record, the school record — carries continuous motivation. Records belong to circles and places, never to persons.

**Count down (the net).** Adversarial B-Sey inverts the scoreboard: each side begins at a start number (§6) and every rally won *subtracts* one from the winner's remaining count. **First to zero wins** — first to nothing. The arithmetic is identical to counting up; the symbolism is its inversion, and a scoreboard is nothing but symbolism: the winning team is the team that first gives away everything it started with. Rally structure follows volleyball (serve rotation; a point every rally). Two refinements complete the mode:

- **The last-gift rule.** A side's final point — the one from 1 to 0 — must be *actively won*: earned by that side's successful play, never conferred by an opponent's unforced error. No match may end on a serve into the net; the anticlimax that deflates conventional rally scoring at its most important moment is ruled out. (Darts' double-out finish is the honest precedent: the insight that endings must be earned is old; its application to rally sport is claimed here.) Classification of a final rally as actively-won is the umpire seat's call, held to the same one-sentence-explainability as every other judgment in the system.
- **No deuce.** Conventional win-by-two exists to prevent flukish endings between equal teams; B-Sey's endings are already guarded by the last-gift rule, and its equality is already engineered at the start line. Adding deuce on top would re-lengthen games unpredictably and dilute the countdown's clean terminus at zero.

**Both arrows, one ruleset.** Cooperative sports count up; adversarial sports count up; darts and little else count down. A single sport in which the *mode selects the arrow* — building in the circle, emptying at the net — is, to the authors' knowledge, novel, and is claimed as architecture (Claim 5): the two arrows are not a gimmick but the two halves of the institution's founding game (a children's balance game in which kindness counts up and gratitude counts down and the ideal score is zero), expressed as sport.

### 5.1 · The scoring ladder, and the implied point

Scoring in a called sport admits an architecture unavailable to every other game, and it follows from one observation: **the caller has already assigned identity.** Every other sport must determine *who* touched the object; this one *chose* who touches it next. What remains to be established is only whether the touch occurred — a binary event, not a localization problem.

Three tiers implement that, and each degrades gracefully into the one below:

```
   TIER       MECHANISM                                    HARDWARE
   ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
   default    the point is IMPLIED by the call —           none
              assume the dyad completed
   validated  k-of-N vote across the circle's own          none
              phone microphones: "was there a strike
              in this window?"
   confirmed  accelerometer in the object reports          instrumented
              {object_id, touch, t}                        object
```

**Sensing never gates play.** A circle with one phone and a rolled sock plays the whole sport; instrumentation improves fidelity and nothing else.

The **microphone tier** deserves note because it is cheap for a non-obvious reason. Localizing a strike acoustically across phones — time-difference-of-arrival — is a research problem: it needs tight clock synchronization and degrades badly in wind and traffic. **Detecting** one does not. A strike is a sharp transient, separable from ambient noise on a single microphone; a **k-of-N agreement rule** across the circle tolerates a phone in a pocket, a gust, and a passing motorcycle; and the vote requires only a coarse shared window, which the mesh's announcement synchronization (§7) already supplies. The reduction from localization to detection is what moves this tier from research to implementation, and it is available *only* because the caller supplies identity.

**The implied point is an epistemic claim, not a semantic one — and the distinction is load-bearing.** The scoring unit remains what §5 defines: a completed delivery-and-reception pair, credited to no individual. The default tier does not redefine the point; it **assumes the dyad completed and permits a human, or later a sensor, to say otherwise.** An implementation that treated the *call itself* as the scoring event would have quietly built a different game — one in which the caller scores — and would have destroyed the dyadic unit that §5 claims. The caller never scores.

**Why such loose epistemics are licensed here.** Because of §8: no point mints, moves, or gates value. **A score that moves nothing requires no audit.** Were the score coupled to money, the instrumented tier would be mandatory and the default tier negligent; because it is not, trust is the correct default and sensing is an amenity. This is a case of a constraint adopted for dignity reasons removing an engineering dependency — the play/currency wall paying for itself in hardware.

One consequence for the record architecture (§12, claim 10): a trusted-by-default streak can silently inflate. **Records therefore carry their evidence tier** — *unvalidated*, *microphone-validated*, or *object-confirmed* — so that a circle's record states not only its magnitude but how it was witnessed.

### 5.2 · The three speaker formats, and the acoustic inversion

A circle has **one acoustic channel and two candidates for it**: the name of the next receiver, and the count. They cannot both be the headline. Interleaving them — *"Sokha! … fourteen! … Dara! … fifteen!"* — buries the name in numerals, and the name is what the sport is for.

The specification therefore inverts the convention of every scoring sport in existence:

> **Every other scoring sport calls the score. This one calls the person.**

The formats below are **named rather than numbered**, deliberately: one constraint stated at the end of this section must survive any future reordering of the list, and a rule that says *"format 1"* breaks the moment a format is inserted ahead of it.

**Relay (the default, and the format a first deployment should ship).** The system speaks the name **privately to a single human relay** — through that person's own device, audibly to them alone — and that person calls it aloud to the circle. Selection remains machine-held, so every distribution property of §4 survives intact; what changes is only who moves the air. The consequences are larger than the modification: there is **no loudspeaker, no mesh, no clock alignment, and no requirement that participants own devices**, so a single borrowed handset serves an entire circle. That matters most exactly where the sport is most wanted — a school that prohibits phones, a park with no signal, a group in which one person has a device.

Two further properties were not the reason for the arrangement and are now the reason to keep it. First, the relay **hears the participant-approved pronunciation immediately before producing it**, which makes correct articulation a *modeled repetition* rather than a reading task; a name read off a screen is mangled precisely for the names this design exists to protect. Second, and more consequential: **the relay produces names aloud, where a player only ever hears them.** The role is therefore where the sport's stated purpose is actually practised, and it follows that the role must **rotate under the same equal-yet-random assignment that distributes receptions**. Left to the circle, the confident participant holds the device and the quiet one never says a name — picked-last rebuilt through the back door of the very role meant to distribute the practice. Rotation also disposes of the objection that the relay is excluded from play: the role is a turn, not a sacrifice. The honest cost is a **tempo ceiling** — the repeat beat is absorbed at long lead times and binds at short ones — which is what the synchronized mesh (§7) is *for*, as a later upgrade rather than a launch prerequisite.

**Call — the name format, spoken to the circle.** The system addresses the whole circle directly over the mesh, and **counts silently.** The count is *visible, not audible*: the mesh already places a screen in every pocket, and adversarial mode's countdown must remain readable for tension and tactics. Silence is not concealment. Numbers are spoken only at the rationed celebration thresholds of §6, which means **a spoken number is always a rite** rather than ambient noise. The chant, if the circle wants one, remains entirely human — the system never leads it and never competes with it. *An accessibility setting must permit a spoken count, off by default, for players who cannot read the screen; the pre-announcement of §4 is already an auditory pre-cue and the same constituency is served by both.*

**Free — the count format, with the caller off.** The caller is disabled entirely; the system counts aloud *with* the circle, and nobody is told where to pass. This is free play with a shared, trusted counter — and it exists because the design's central mechanism is unproven (§9, §11). It is also **why the microphone tier is specified**: with no call, the system has nothing to count from unless it *hears* the strike. The two are one feature.

The formats are **mutually exclusive by construction** — a session runs exactly one — which resolves the apparent contradiction among them. The rule was never that numbers are undesirable; it is that **the name must never compete with numerals** — one voice per channel, and the channel's content follows what the mode is for.

**Three constraints on Free, because it switches the sport's dignity guarantees off.** Equal-touch distribution and the cannot-target-weakness property both live *in the caller*; with the caller off, humans choose receivers again and the picked-last dynamic returns. Accordingly: **(a)** a caller-on format remains the default everywhere; **(b)** Free must never be represented to an institution as equivalent — the anti-exclusion properties an administrator approves are precisely the caller's, and a free-play session does not carry them; **(c)** **any trial of the caller mechanism must run a caller-on format** — Relay or Call — or it measures nothing. That constraint is stated by *property* rather than by position in this list, which is why the formats carry names: a future revision may add or reorder formats, and the rule must not silently come to designate the wrong one. On which point see §9.1: Free is not merely a fallback but the natural **control arm** for the experiment that tests the caller itself.

## 6 · The Start Line: Handicaps as Sizes, Not Ratings

Mixed-ability play fails two ways: with no handicap, the game is a rout; with a visible skill-rating handicap, the game is fair and *humiliating* — the rating announces precisely how much worse you are, to two decimal places, forever. Golf's handicap system, the great success of the genre, threads the needle partly by its own crudeness and its social conventions; explicitly skill-rated matchmaking (chess Elo, ranked ladders in esports) threads it in the opposite direction, by making the rating the point. A family sport needs a third path.

**The start-line handicap.** In net mode, the two sides may begin at *different* start numbers — the stronger side at 33, the beginners at 21; first to zero still wins. The handicap is public, singular, and ceremonial: one number per side, announced before play, arguable by nobody because chosen by no player (the system sets it, and its rubric is one-sentence-explainable: *fairness plus desired game length*). It is applied **at the start line, never in the stream**: once play begins, the caller and umpire treat both sides identically (§4's hard constraint), and any correction of a misjudged handicap waits for the *next* game — the rubber band lives between games, never inside one. A mid-game rubber band (catch-up mechanics, dynamic difficulty) is the standard videogame solution and is rejected here by name: dynamic mercy is detectable, and detected mercy is humiliation delivered by an algorithm.

**Quantization-for-dignity.** Start numbers are drawn from a coarse fixed lattice: **multiples of three only** (3, 6, 9, … 21, … 33). The coarseness is the point. If start numbers could be any integer, the pair (26, 24) would communicate a *precision measurement* — a skill rating in countdown clothing, recreating exactly what the start-line design exists to avoid. Quantized to the lattice, the handicap can only say what a garment size says: roughly this, roughly that, next size up. The minimum expressible gap (three) is large enough to be read as a category rather than a diagnosis. Handicap literature treats coarseness as a deficiency of precision; this design claims the opposite — **in social play, quantization is a dignity mechanism** — and registers the inversion as a claim (Claim 4). Two free properties follow: the start number doubles as the **duration dial** (a park quickie from 9, a festival final from 33), and the lattice happens to be the institution's own numerological signature (three, the heart; thirty-three, the founding number) — a brand fact noted for honesty and carrying no mechanism weight.

## 7 · The Mesh and the Ball

**The synced-circle mesh.** The call must be heard outdoors, over wind and traffic, by everyone at once — the mundane detail on which the whole design stands or falls in a real park. The solution is already standing in the circle: **every player's phone joins the session and speaks the call in unison.** N players are N loudspeakers arranged, by the game's own geometry, in a ring around the play — a surround system that assembles itself and scales with the circle. The mesh deletes the alternative's failure modes (a single device at the circle's center is a trip hazard, a theft target, a single point of failure, and one weak speaker), and it enables a property no single device could offer: **the multilingual circle**. The *call* — the name — is one utterance in one voice; but score announcements and celebrations can be rendered per device, in each phone's own language. A grandmother hears the milestone in Khmer as her grandchild hears it in English, in the same circle at the same moment. For diaspora families — the institution's first constituency — the feature is not cosmetic; it is the difference between a game the whole family plays and a game the grandchildren play while the elders watch. The engineering cost is stated honestly: multi-device simultaneous playback requires tight clock synchronization or the call smears into echo; local time-sync is a solved class of problem, but it is version one's hardest component.

**The two-stage hardware split.** Version one of B-Sey is **software plus any ball on earth** — the app is the caller, umpire, and equalizer, and the ball is whatever the circle owns. Nothing in §3–6 requires instrumented equipment. The manufactured **B-Sey™ ball** — designed for the foot-and-paddle dual strike, and eventually voiced, so the call emanates from the play itself — follows as a second-stage artifact, and brings with it the **ball chronicle**: the ball carries a durable identity (per this corpus's Proof of Coordinate℠ publication) and keeps its own biography — the circles it has played, the records it has witnessed, the dedications spoken over it. The chronicle records *collective* history only (a ball remembers parks and streaks, never individual performance), and a well-traveled ball becomes what sporting goods have never been: a commons artifact whose value grows with use, passed between circles as a gift that carries its own story. The split is a deliberate adoption strategy: the sport must never gate on hardware; the hardware, when it comes, must only deepen a game already alive.

### 7.1 · Local-first architecture, and the object's actual job

**The entire game runs on one device, offline, with no accounts.** Names, pronunciations, pacing, both scoring arrows, start numbers, and celebration thresholds are local state; the complete data model of a thirty-player circle with recorded pronunciations is on the order of a megabyte. Only genuinely shared artifacts require a network — cross-circle discovery, records that travel between places, and the object's own biography.

This is not primarily an economy measure. It is the architectural expression of constraints the design already carries: no per-player statistics, no analytics on minors, chosen names never leaving the circle, and a private-mirror rule for any per-player derivation. Each of those independently says *keep it on the device*. That the result is also nearly free to operate at any scale, and that it works in a park with no signal, a school with a locked-down network, and a village on intermittent data, is a coincidence of goods rather than a trade-off.

Two engineering notes. The synchronized announcement requires **device-to-device transport, not a server** — the platform peer-to-peer facilities (Apple's MultipeerConnectivity; Android's Nearby Connections) are designed for exactly this. The genuine risks are **cross-platform pairing**, since any real circle is mixed, and **clock alignment**, since simultaneous playback across N devices reads as an echo rather than one voice beyond roughly twenty milliseconds of skew; a leader device scheduling playback at a negotiated offset is the standard remedy, with **single-announcer fallback** — never a deliberate stagger — if alignment resists. And the classroom deployment of §9.1 requires no mesh at all, so the hardest component is not on the critical path to the design's primary channel.

**The object's job is smaller than it appears.** Because the caller assigns identity (§5.1), an instrumented object need not report *where* it is or *who* holds it. Radio-signal-strength proximity cannot in any case discriminate between players standing one to three metres apart with bodies intervening; precise ranging radios exist but are confined to premium handsets, which would breach the design's zero-floor commitment. **The object therefore needs an accelerometer and a clock, and uses its radio as a messenger rather than as a tape measure** — reporting `{object_id, touch, t}` for the system to pair with the call it has just issued. The consequence is that instrumentation costs the price of a motion sensor rather than a positioning system, and that **the object needs no speaker at all**, since the mesh already speaks — deleting the most expensive and least durable component from an object designed to be kicked.

Two constraints follow that an implementer will not derive on their own. First, an object broadcasting a stable identifier is a **tracking beacon**, and children are the primary population; the identifier must rotate publicly while the object's durable identity is held cryptographically and locally, so that the biography of claim 10 survives without the object becoming a tag on a child. Second, and more subtly: **once touch events exist and the system knows who was called, a per-player success table becomes trivially computable** — the exact artifact §4 and §12 forbid. The guard must therefore be restated at the hardware layer: **object telemetry aggregates to the circle, never to a player scoreboard.** The general lesson is worth stating plainly for anyone instrumenting a sport: *adding a sensor can silently re-open a guarantee that was previously enforced by the absence of data.*

## 8 · What Winning Moves: The Play/Currency Wall

Stated as the shared spine of this sprint and applied here in its simplest form: **competition and play may generate the story; only witnessed giving moves the currency.** In B-Sey:

- Victory in net mode moves **story and honor**: the chronicle records it; the winning side's captain may speak; the family retells it. It moves nothing else. No points convert to anything; no aura, reputation, ranking, or economic value attaches to winning; there are no ladders, no seasons, no standings.
- The streak moves **story**: a number attached to a circle and a place, celebrated at repdigits, retold as local legend.
- Where the sport meets the institution's gift economy at all — the challenge-and-stakes wrapper in which a match is played *for* somewhere money should go — every mechanism belongs to the companion paper (*The Wager That Isn't*), and the division is clean: that paper's walls guarantee that even there, **no money anywhere is contingent on outcome in its existence**; outcomes touch only honor and the address of already-given gifts. The sport itself never touches value.
- **No player statistics exist.** This is the wall's deepest application, and it is absolute rather than default-off: the system does not compute individual performance records at all, because a computed-but-hidden statistic is a breach waiting for a product manager. What the scarcity era recorded by necessity (§1), the abundance era must refuse by design — total witness makes *everything* recordable, and the whole dignity architecture of this sport rests on recording the circle and not the person.

An extractive implementation of this paper's mechanisms — an AI caller with engagement-optimized distribution, player-skill surveillance, streak leaderboards, and rated matchmaking — is entirely feasible and is the obvious product a games incumbent would build from the same parts. The claims below are published precisely so that the *dignity-preserving* configuration is prior art: the caller that never evaluates, the handicap that cannot humiliate, the record that has no names in it.

## 9 · Adoption Thesis and Pre-Registered Predictions

A sport's mechanisms are worthless if nobody plays. The design's adoption thesis is stated in one line for the record: B-Sey's path to scale is the **utility path** — it is the only sport that solves a non-sport problem (strangers becoming friends; names being learned; nobody excluded), so institutions that adopt *tools* (schools, workplaces, festivals, parks departments) can carry it to places marketing never reaches. Its floor is as low as any game on earth (any ball, any space, a free app, rules learned in three seconds because the call grammar — *"Sey says…"* — is Simon Says, which every child already knows); its mixed-age property is pickleball's growth engine, engineered threefold; its streak is a native short-video format; and its ceiling — the acrobatic kick — is inherited from sepak takraw, whose brutal floor kept a spectacular sport regional and whose lesson (spectacle recruits, access retains) this design encodes as the paddle. The full ranked analysis lives in the institution's design canon; the sprint paper registers only the wager and its instruments.

The predictions, pre-registered at the design layer (n = 0; every instrument awaits the first pilots):

- **P-S1 (the name test).** After a single first session of at least 20 minutes in a circle of 5+ mutual strangers, a majority of participants can freely recall the chosen names of a majority of the circle. This is the mechanism's core social claim made falsifiable; if name-recall does not materially exceed the mixer-conversation baseline, the sport's central marketing truth is false and will be retired.
- **P-S2 (the friendship test).** Among mutual-stranger dyads who meet in a circle, a measurably higher share exchange contact or co-attend a subsequent circle within 30 days than the baseline for comparable park co-presence without the game. The third-place thesis, instrumented.
- **P-S3 (the fun wager — publish either way).** The design's existential risk is named in §11: being *told* where to pass may read as magic or as micromanagement. Prediction: in preference trials, caller-on play is chosen for replay at least as often as caller-off free play in mixed-ability circles (the design predicts the caller *wins* precisely where ability is mixed, and may lose in homogeneous expert circles, which is acceptable and expected). The authors commit to publishing this result whichever way it falls, including the design revision it forces if it falls badly.

### 9.1 · The icebreaker slot, the classroom, and the recess test

The adoption thesis above ranks schools fifth. On the analysis below they rank first, and the reason is a general one worth stating separately from this sport.

**A product that claims to ride an existing behaviour must name a behaviour a target adult actually performed, unprompted, in the last twelve months** — and the host must also match the product's commitment level and be in good standing with whoever must permit it. Run that test across this sport's candidate channels and exactly one host passes all three parts. Park circles are a *new* habit: nobody currently goes to a park to play a called circle game, so that channel must be budgeted as habit formation. The classroom is different. Every teacher on earth faces the same two problems in the same week of every year — learn thirty names, and include every child — and *already runs a name game to solve them*, unprompted, annually, worldwide. Elementary morning-greeting routines are a standing institutional ritual. Recess is daily. The commitment shape matches almost exactly: ten to fifteen minutes, free, no equipment, teacher-led, repeatable. And the standing is positive rather than merely tolerated: name learning, inclusion, and anti-bullying are things administrators actively want, which is the inverse of the polarity most novel objects carry into a school.

**The reframe this forces is the practical conclusion of the whole paper.** The behaviour this sport attaches to is not *sport* at all; it is **the name-learning icebreaker** — performed at school openings, orientations, retreats, onboardings, receptions, and newcomer groups, universally, and universally disliked. Its incumbent competitors are the seated round of self-introductions and the disclosure game. Against those, a called circle wins on every axis that matters: **twenty exposures to each name instead of one**, movement instead of standing, nothing to disclose about oneself, and no performance in front of strangers. **The sport ships as an icebreaker and is discovered as a sport** — and Friendship mode at long lead time (§3.1, §4.1) *is* that icebreaker, arrived at from the sport's own logic rather than designed for the channel.

**Rosterless classroom mode.** The classroom deployment is specified as **single-device**: one teacher's device speaks; no child device participates; the roster is entered once by the teacher and reused; **no accounts exist for minors, no analytics are collected, nothing is uploaded, and the roster is local and erasable.** This is not compliance decoration — a roster of children's names and voices is precisely the artifact that fails an institutional privacy review, and the permitting authority and the privacy authority are the same office. It also happens to remove the mesh, the network, the accounts, and the instrumented object from the primary channel: **a teacher, a phone, and a soft sack.**

The pronunciation commitment (§4, claim 9) is satisfied in this mode **without recording anything**. At long lead the called child raises a hand and says their own name, and the circle repeats it; **the scaffold is itself the pronunciation-capture mechanism, performed by a human.** The circle converges on the child's own delivery rather than a synthesizer's approximation — which is the correct outcome in the venue where mispronunciation is actually inflicted, and which requires no biometric capture to achieve.

**Two further pre-registrations.** The design's existential uncertainty (§11) is whether being *told* where to pass is delightful or officious, and only play answers it. The classroom supplies an unusually clean and cheap instrument:

- **P-S4 (name acquisition).** A circle that reaches the short-lead band will show ≥90% correct-name *and* correct-pronunciation recall among its members at one week, measured by naming photographs. This refines P-S1's instrument rather than replacing it.
- **P-S5 (the recess test — the fun test made falsifiable; publish either way).** Within two weeks of teacher-led sessions in a caller-on format, at least one **student-organized circle will form at recess without adult prompting**, and at least one such circle will include children from a different classroom. The prediction carries a **stated precondition**: a single device must be *available* at recess and no instruction to use it may be given. Without that precondition a null result is uninterpretable — it cannot distinguish *the game is not fun* from *there was no phone*, which is the one distinction the test exists to make; with it, the readout is clean in both directions. The second clause is the stronger instrument: cross-class mixing is this design's social claim, measured in the one building where the social graph is otherwise frozen by classroom assignment.

P-S5 costs one teacher, one class, one sack, and two weeks, and its readout cannot be spun: unsupervised children do not organize a game out of politeness. **Free (§5.2) supplies the control arm** — a second class playing free play with the same object and the same counter. The comparison isolates the caller itself, which is the single variable the entire design rests on and the one no amount of design reasoning can settle.

### 9.2 · Who pays: place sponsorship, and the test that scopes it

The economics follow the architecture. Because §7.1 puts the game on the device, the marginal cost of a player is approximately zero, and only the shared layer — cross-circle discovery, travelling records, organizer tooling — has a server behind it. The free tier is therefore *the entire sport*, permanently, and revenue must come from somewhere that is not the player.

The specification is **place sponsorship**: a patron funds the shared layer for a **geofenced public place** — a school, a park, a neighbourhood — and every circle that plays there receives it. The individual player never pays, at any tier, ever.

**The scoping test is non-excludability, and it is the whole rule:**

> **A place is sponsorable if and only if the sponsor cannot exclude anyone from it.**

Not *does the sponsor benefit* — they always do, and should — but *could the sponsor shut the gate*. A private residence fails: the door is controlled, the good is excludable, and the payment is a purchase wearing a patron's coat. A neighbourhood, park, school, plaza, or temple courtyard passes: the good is non-excludable, so the payment remains a gift even when the sponsor's own family plays there.

Four constraints are specified with it, and the first is the one an implementer will get wrong:

1. **No commercial sponsorship of child-facing places.** Schools and children's circles are funded by individuals, families, alumni, parent associations, or civic groups — or by nobody. Commercial patrons may sponsor adult venues and public parks. This eliminates the child-advertising problem structurally rather than managing it, which is the only treatment that survives contact with the world's various child-marketing regimes.
2. **Acknowledgment, never advertising.** Where a sponsor is shown at all: a **name, never a message** — no slogan, claim, link, or animation — and placed on the organizer's surface or in the session's closing thanks, never in the children's play surface.
3. **No control, no data, no exclusivity.** A sponsor learns nothing about who played or how often, may not be exclusive to a public place, and gains no influence over it; **an unsponsored place is never degraded**, since the free tier is the whole game. Reach reporting is specifically prohibited: an impressions metric reintroduces both advertising logic and child data by the back door.
4. **The two flows never net.** The sponsorship payment is revenue; any gratitude the circles direct back at the sponsor is not a rebate against it. Consistent with this corpus's routing rule — *thanks may fund a livelihood; thanks may never pay a bill* (see *B-Links: Proof-of-Humanity-Signed Shareable Provenance*, §6.1, where the rule is specified and freed) — value returned to a sponsor is credited as **forward-spendable gift capacity**, never as a discount and never as withdrawable funds. What a thanked sponsor receives is the capacity to give again.

The resulting circuit is worth naming because it is the reason this revenue model is not a compromise with the mission: **a patron funds a place, the circles that play there thank the patron, and the thanks funds the patron's next gift.** The payment mechanism is itself an instance of the thing the institution builds.

## 10 · Prior Art, Engaged Generously

**The circle games.** The direct ancestors are the Southeast Asian circle keep-alive family: the Khmer *sey*, played at dusk in courtyards and parks across Cambodia; Myanmar's *chinlone*, a six-person circle game explicitly organized around beauty and collective flow rather than opposition — the world's great existing proof that non-adversarial ball sport can sustain a culture for centuries; China's *jianzi* and the global hacky-sack/footbag family. B-Sey is a descendant of this lineage and says so in its name; what the lineage lacks — and what kept it local — is an equalization layer (the skill floor of foot-only play is high) and any addressing mechanism (circles of strangers stay strangers). Sepak takraw contributes the net form and the spectacular ceiling, and the cautionary lesson of a magnificent sport whose difficulty floor confined it to its region.

**The designed prosocial sports.** Sport design as deliberate social engineering has a distinguished small lineage: korfball (1902), which mandated mixed-sex teams by rule; tchoukball (1970), designed by Hermann Brandt from an explicit medical-humanist brief to eliminate aggression — its no-interference rule is the closest ancestor of the cannot-target-weakness property, though it removes *contact* rather than *targeting*; kin-ball (1986), designed for total participation with its all-touch service rule — the nearest prior art to equal distribution, achieved by static rule rather than adaptive caller; Special Olympics Unified Sports and the walking-football/walking-netball movement, which achieve mixed-ability play by restricting the able rather than equalizing the field. The Ultimate frisbee community's spirit-of-the-game — self-officiating as ethos — is the officiating seat's spiritual ancestor; B-Sey completes what spirit-of-the-game gestures at by making the neutral judge structural.

**The handicap systems.** Golf's handicap (formalized from the 18th century) is the great proof that start-line equalization can carry a mixed-ability sport for centuries; go's fixed-stone handicaps and chess odds-giving are older still. All are lattice-coarse in practice, none (to the authors' knowledge) has claimed coarseness as a *dignity* mechanism, and none operates in a team net sport. Videogame dynamic-difficulty adjustment and matchmaking ratings are the anti-examples engaged in §6: fairness delivered as either hidden mid-stream mercy or public skill measurement, the two failure modes the start-line design refuses.

**The caller's ancestors.** Simon Says supplies the call grammar — a named authority directing named players — as a children's commons centuries old; square-dance calling is the adult form: a caller directing the figures of a cooperative physical game in real time, and the closest single ancestor of the caller seat (what square dance does with figures, B-Sey does with receivers). Neither selects *whom* adaptively; neither equalizes; both prove the pleasure of called play.

**The instrumented-sport patent field.** Smart balls (Adidas miCoach, Wilson X and successors), computer-vision officiating (Hawk-Eye, VAR, automated ball-strike systems), and AI sports-coaching applications (HomeCourt and the computer-vision training genre) constitute an active commercial and patent field moving fast in precisely the opposite direction from this paper: toward finer individual performance measurement. The field is engaged respectfully as the reason this publication exists now: an AI-directed pass-calling mechanism with adaptive distribution is an obvious near-future patent claim in extractive form (engagement-optimized, stat-generating), and the present publication places the mechanism class — and specifically its dignity-preserving configuration — in the public domain first.

**The third-place literature.** Oldenburg's *The Great Good Place* and Putnam's *Bowling Alone* frame the vanishing social infrastructure the sport addresses; the recent public-health literature on loneliness (including the 2023 U.S. Surgeon General's advisory) supplies the epidemiological register. Bowling leagues were Putnam's emblem of what was lost; a self-assembling park circle that teaches names is offered, without irony, as a candidate replacement part.

## 11 · Honest Limits

*This section is deliberately free of lineage, resonance, and metaphor.*

1. **n = 0.** The sport is unbuilt and unplayed. Every property claimed above is architectural; no prediction has data; the first real circle may falsify any of them.
2. **The fun risk is existential and unresolved.** Directed play may feel like magic in mixed circles and like micromanagement among skilled peers. If caller-on play is not preferred for replay (P-S3), the central mechanism fails as a product regardless of its virtues as architecture. Free-play mode and caller-cadence tuning are the mitigation paths; neither is designed yet.
3. **The mesh is unproven engineering.** Simultaneous multi-device audio requires clock synchronization tight enough to avoid echo, on heterogeneous consumer phones, outdoors, on degraded networks. This is version one's hardest component and it sits directly on the critical path of the first minute of a first session.
4. **App dependency taxes the floor.** Pickup soccer needs no battery, account, or download. Every point of onboarding friction spends the zero-floor advantage; offline operation and caller-optional play must be real, not roadmap.
5. **The equal-random policy has unmodeled edges.** Injured players, players who wish to opt down, players joining mid-streak, very large circles (call latency vs. rally tempo), and adversarial-mode team-size asymmetries all require policy the design has not specified. Each is solvable; none is solved.
6. **Accessibility claims are untested.** The call-before-ball structure plausibly aids low-vision players (an auditory pre-cue) and the circle-plus-paddle geometry plausibly admits wheelchair players; both are stated as design intentions, not verified properties, and will not be marketed until tested with the relevant communities.
7. **Copyability is total.** The mechanism is a weekend's work for any competent app studio, which is the reason for this publication's timing: the defense is prior art plus the layers no cloner inherits (the mission, the name, the constraint set — and an extractive clone that omits the constraint set is, by that omission, visibly not this sport).
8. **Base rates are brutal.** Global pastimes are a power-law lottery; pickleball's overnight success took fifty years. The design's honest consolation is that its unit of success is one park: a single circle that runs produces its friendships on the spot, whether or not the world follows.
9. **The three-axis parameterization multiplies untested surface.** Twenty-seven combinations of method, mode, and level are legal, and none has been played. Mixed-method circles in particular assume that a single soft object serves catch, paddle, and foot without one method spoiling the rally for the others — plausible, unverified, and the kind of thing one afternoon in a park would settle or destroy.
10. **The implied-point tier will inflate records.** Trusting the call means a dropped object sometimes counts, and social correction in a circle is real but imperfect. Evidence tiering (§5.1) makes the inflation legible rather than eliminating it, and no cross-place record comparison should be published without its tier.
11. **The classroom channel depends on a privacy posture that has not met an actual reviewer.** §9.1 specifies local-only operation because that is what such reviews normally require, but no district has yet examined this product, requirements vary by jurisdiction, and an unfavourable determination in a large market would remove the channel this paper ranks first.
12. **Free can mask the failure it was built to survive.** A caller-optional mode is the honest mitigation for the fun risk and also a way to never learn the answer: if circles quietly migrate to free play, usage will look healthy while the central mechanism goes untested. The pre-registration in §9.1 is written to force the comparison rather than permit the drift.
13. **The sponsorship model is unexercised and its guards are unpoliced.** No place has been sponsored. The child-facing commercial prohibition (§9.2) is a rule this institution can hold in its own implementation and cannot enforce in anyone else's — which is, as with the constraint set generally, exactly the part a cloner will drop first.

## 12 · Claims

The following mechanisms and architectures, individually and in combination, are published as prior art and dedicated to the public domain:

1. **The caller mechanism.** A physical game in which an automated intermediary announces, before each play, the identity of the required next receiver, audibly and by name, such that play cannot proceed except through the named player.
2. **The cannot-target-weakness property.** A net or team sport in which the automated caller designates receivers on all sides, structurally removing opponent-selection (including weakest-player targeting) from the space of legal or possible plays, and confining skill expression to delivery quality.
3. **Equal-yet-random distribution.** A caller selection policy jointly guaranteeing (a) equal per-player reception counts over a session and (b) unpredictable selection order, with the explicit constraint that the policy never conditions on player skill, performance, or match state during play.
4. **The start-line handicap with quantization-for-dignity.** Team equalization implemented solely as asymmetric starting scores in a countdown game, drawn from a coarse fixed lattice (exemplarily, multiples of three), set by a neutral intermediary from a fairness-plus-duration rubric, with between-game (never within-game) recalibration — the coarseness claimed specifically as an anti-rating, dignity-preserving mechanism.
5. **The dual-arrow ruleset.** A single sport in which cooperative mode scores by ascending collective streak and adversarial mode scores by countdown to zero ("first to nothing wins"), with mode selecting the arrow.
6. **The dyadic scoring unit.** A scoring rule under which the atomic scoring event is a completed delivery-and-reception pair credited to no individual, such that no player can score alone.
7. **The last-gift rule.** A countdown finishing rule requiring the final point to be actively won by the finishing side, excluding conferral by opponent unforced error, as classified by the automated umpire.
8. **The synced-circle mesh.** Delivery of game announcements through simultaneous clock-synchronized playback on all participating players' personal devices, arranged by the game's own geometry as a surround array, with per-device language rendering of non-call announcements.
9. **The name-learning mechanic with chosen-name privacy.** The use of pre-play name announcement as a deliberate stranger-introduction mechanism, bound to user-chosen circle names (never legal names), with children's circles restricted to first names or nicknames; including double-blind surfacing of nearby solitary individuals as circle invitees such that neither party learns who initiated and no solitude status is ever disclosed.
10. **The collective-only chronicle.** A witness architecture that records streaks, records, and celebrations as properties of circles, places, and equipment (including a ball-borne biography anchored to a durable device identity), while computing no individual performance statistics whatsoever; with celebration rationing on an exponential repdigit schedule.
11. **The call-lead-time ladder with scaffold fading.** A difficulty parameter defined as the interval between the naming of the receiver and the object's arrival — invariant to participant count and to method — banded such that the longest band includes an explicit identification scaffold (the called player self-identifies by raised hand and may speak their own name) which becomes physically unavailable as the interval shortens; so that advancement through the bands tracks the group's *name recall* rather than physical skill.
12. **Method as declared start-line handicap.** Heterogeneous body vocabularies (hand-catch, implement, foot) exercised by different participants within a single circle on a single shared object, each self-declared publicly before play as an instance of the start-line handicap of claim 4, with reception-by-catch bounded by a release interval clocked to the caller's own cadence.
13. **The mode ladder with a no-score entry rung.** A single sport whose modes are ordered by opponent relation — none, own record, other circle — whose entry mode has no scoring of any kind, and whose advancement between modes is elected by the participants rather than triggered by performance.
14. **Circle-scoped adaptation, and the circle-scoped level invariant.** Any automatic adjustment of pacing or difficulty conditions solely on aggregate state of the whole circle, never on per-participant response latency or performance, with per-participant timing neither stored, displayed, ranked, nor retained; the level being a property of the circle and never of a player.
15. **The three-tier scoring architecture with the implied point and evidence tiering.** Scoring by (a) inference from the call alone, (b) k-of-N agreement across participants' device microphones detecting — not localizing — a strike within a window supplied by the announcement synchronization, or (c) a motion sensor in the object reporting a touch event paired to the outstanding call; identity in all tiers supplied by the caller rather than by sensing; the object's radio used as an event messenger rather than a ranging device; records annotated with the evidence tier that produced them; and the acoustic inversion by which the caller speaks participant names aloud while the count is rendered visually, with an alternate caller-off format in which the system counts aloud and designates no receiver.
16. **Rosterless local-only deployment for minors, and the non-excludability test for place sponsorship.** A single-device deployment in which participant names are entered locally by a supervising adult, no accounts exist for minors, no telemetry leaves the device, and pronunciation is taught by the named participant in person rather than captured as biometric data; together with a place-scoped patronage model funding a shared service layer for a geofenced location, admissible only where the patron cannot exclude anyone from that location, and constrained by prohibitions on commercial patronage of child-facing places, on any sponsor message beyond a name, on reach reporting, and on netting patron gratitude against the patron's own payment.
17. **The determined caller, with count-only circle coherence and the seat-migration bar.** A called sport in which the receiver sequence is a deterministic pure function of the participant roster and a seed declared before play, specified as a cross-platform bit-exact contract with published conformance vectors, such that (a) any participant may independently recompute a completed session and verify the equal-yet-random distribution property, rendering distribution fairness auditable rather than asserted; (b) multiple devices in one circle stay in agreement by exchanging only an elapsed-call count — never participant identities, sequence state, or selection reasons — with rejoin by fast-forward and no server; and (c) no per-participant record exists to be disclosed or repurposed, because the generator retains none. Claimed together with the governing partition: **determined** seats (caller, equalizer), whose outputs are checkable by recomputation and which never condition on evaluated player state, and **judged** seats (umpire, witness), whose outputs are checkable only by one-sentence explanation and which hold no authority over receiver selection; including the constraint that instrumentation of the object or environment may supply evidence to the judged seats alone.
18. **The host-agnostic turn-assignment layer.** The caller primitive of claims 1–3 — pre-announced named-turn assignment, equal-yet-random distribution, circle-scoped adaptation, and the absence of per-participant evaluation — applied to an **arbitrary host activity** rather than to any particular sport: the warm-up, drill, or rotation phase of a conventional sport; a rehearsal; a classroom, workplace, or conference gathering; or any bounded group activity in which turns must be distributed among named participants. Claimed independently of the ruleset of §§3–6, so that the mechanism's applicability is not confined to the circle game specified here, and expressly including its use as a preliminary segment preceding an unrelated primary activity.
19. **The relay call — decoupled selection and articulation.** A called activity in which the automated intermediary announces the selected participant's name **privately to a single human relay** — through that person's own device, audibly to them alone — who then articulates it aloud to the assembled group, such that: (a) selection fairness is machine-held while acoustic delivery is human, preserving every distribution property of claims 1–3 without the system addressing the group directly; (b) the activity runs on **one device**, with no loudspeaker, no audio infrastructure, and no device-to-device synchronization, and therefore no dependence on network, clock alignment, or participant device ownership; (c) the relay hears the participant-approved pronunciation immediately before producing it, making correct articulation a **modeled repetition** rather than a reading task, and thereby recruiting the relay's own vocal production rather than recognition alone; and (d) the relay role is itself assigned by the same equal-yet-random process that assigns receivers and rotates among participants, so that articulation practice is distributed rather than captured by the most confident participant, and so that occupying the role is a turn rather than an exclusion from play.

## 13 · Lineage, and the Close

The lineage of this design is a courtyard at dusk. Across Cambodia, in the hour when the heat breaks, people stand in circles and keep a small woven ball in the air — no teams, no referee, no score that anyone writes down, only the shared project of *not letting it drop*. The game survived what Cambodia's twentieth century did to everything else that was gentle. This paper's whole mechanism is, in one reading, an attempt to give that circle three things the modern world took from its conditions of possibility: a fair way for the unequal to stand in it together, a voice to welcome strangers into it by name, and a record that remembers the circle rather than sorting its members. The sport is named for that game, and the name performs its own thesis — *sey* (សី), the inherited circle; *say*, the spoken name; a children's call-game hiding inside both. A note on the script, since the pun is a claim about a language and should be checkable in it: **សី** is *sey*, and the mascot who teaches the game is **ទន្សាយ**, *tonsay* — a rabbit. The two are unrelated words that merely share a syllable once romanized, which is worth stating plainly: in Khmer the resemblance is visible as coincidence, and only in Latin letters can it be mistaken for an etymology. The pun is offered as a pun. What the caller calls, in the end, is the oldest social technology there is: *you, here, now — the ball is coming to you.*
