## Abstract

In 2007 Rafael Sorkin, one of the founders of causal set theory, remarked in a footnote that the "accretive time" of his sequential-growth models "seems close to that of C.D. Broad, and also to that of the 'Vibhajavadin' school within the Buddhist philosophical tradition." Fay Dowker, quoting him in 2022, added an invitation: *"A interesting project for a scholar of Buddhism interested in physics would be to try to determine the 'Vibhajavadin' view of time, perception and epistemology in detail by examining the earliest existing records."*

This paper takes up that invitation from the Pāli sources. It finds that **there is no single Vibhajjavādin view of time to report.** The name has at least two referents:

- **In the northern Abhidharma**, the *Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* (ad V.25) defines the Vibhajyavādins as those who hold that **the present exists, and past action whose fruit has not yet been given exists, while ripened past action and the future do not**. This is a view of present-plus-some-past with no future, and it is the Buddhist position nearest to a growing block, and so to Sorkin's accretion.
- **In the Pāli tradition**, Vibhajjavāda is the Mahāvihāra's own name for itself. The commentary on the *Kathāvatthu* tells how the Third Council answered a king's question about the Buddha's doctrine with *"Vibhajjavādī, mahārājā."* That same *Kathāvatthu* **refutes four rival positions on time**: *all exists*, *past aggregates exist*, *some past and some future exist*, and *exists as past*. The third of these includes, for the past, the very view the *Kośa* calls Vibhajyavādin.

So the tradition that uses the name today holds **presentism**: only the present exists. Presentism is exactly the view Sorkin says his model is *not* ("the sole alternative to the 'block universe'", as he puts it, "a doctrine that identifies reality with a three-dimensional instant"). **The Pāli Vibhajjavāda is the foil of causal-set growth, not its twin.**

The genuine point of contact lies elsewhere, and it is narrower:

- **Past action conditions the present without existing.** In the *Paṭṭhāna*'s grammar, this is the kamma condition operating across moments, a reading the sub-commentary makes explicit.
- **A consciousness can take a past object without being objectless** (*Kathāvatthu* IX.6).

This is a claim about influence, not about ordered existence. We state what would falsify each finding, and we keep the physics comparison labelled as a comparison.

**Keywords:** causal set theory, sequential growth, growing block, presentism, eternalism, temporal becoming, Buddhist philosophy of time, Kathāvatthu, Abhidharmakośa, Sarvāstivāda, Vibhajjavāda, Theravāda, Sorkin, Dowker.

## 1 · The Invitation

Causal set theory replaces the spacetime continuum with a locally finite partial order: discrete events, related only by *before*. Sorkin's slogan is *order + number = geometry*. In its sequential-growth ("classical sequential growth") models the causal set is not given all at once; it **grows**, one element at a time, each new element born to the future of some of those already present. Sorkin argued that this growth gives an objective correlate of the passage of time, a becoming that relativity was thought to have ruled out. His 2007 paper, "Relativity theory does not imply that the future already exists: a counterexample," makes the case. In §5 he describes what the growth yields as *"something 'four dimensional' from the very beginning, but which at any stage of its growth is still incomplete"*, and he adds the footnote:

> The notion of "accretive time" that arises here seems close to that of C.D. Broad, and also to that of the "Vibhajavadin" school within the Buddhist philosophical tradition.

Fay Dowker, developing Sorkin's proposal as a response to the hard problem of consciousness (arXiv 2209.07653), quotes the footnote and adds her own:

> A interesting project for a scholar of Buddhism interested in physics would be to try to determine the "Vibhajavadin" view of time, perception and epistemology in detail by examining the earliest existing records.

Her notes point for the Vibhajavādin view to Stcherbatsky's *The Central Conception of Buddhism* (1923), and to his summary of early Buddhism as "no Matter, no Substance, only separate elements, momentary flashes of efficient energy … a flow of existential moments."

The question is well posed, and it is answerable, because the records exist, in Pāli and in Sanskrit, and they are unusually explicit about time. What follows reads them.

*Spelling note.* Sorkin and Dowker write *Vibhajavadin*, quoted as they wrote it. Pāli sources give *Vibhajjavāda*, Sanskrit sources *Vibhajyavāda*. The spellings mark traditions, and this paper keeps them apart deliberately.

## 2 · Who Were the Vibhajjavādins?

### 2.1 · The Pāli self-designation

The *Kathāvatthu* itself, the fifth book of the Abhidhamma Piṭaka, never uses the word. The self-designation is found in its commentary (*Kathāvatthu-aṭṭhakathā*, c. 5th century CE, drawing on older material). The commentary describes the Third Council under Aśoka: the king, having expelled monks of other views, asks the remaining monks what the Buddha taught.

> *"kiṃvādī, bhante, sammāsambuddho"ti? "Vibhajjavādī, mahārājā"ti.*
>
> "Of what doctrine, venerable sir, was the Fully Awakened One?" — "A Vibhajjavādin, great king."

The elder Moggaliputtatissa then composes the *Kathāvatthu* to refute the views that had arisen. In the tradition's own account, therefore, **the *Kathāvatthu* is the Vibhajjavādin record.**

### 2.2 · A name for a group

Cousins (2001) shows that the word has two senses in the Pāli commentarial literature: an epithet of the Buddha (one who answers by analysis, *vibhajja*) and the name of a school. In the second sense it historically named the common ancestor of **four** schools: the Mahiṃsāsakas, the Dhammaguttikas, the Kassapiyas and the Tambapaṇṇiyas, the last being the Mahāvihāra of Sri Lanka whose texts the Pāli canon preserves. The commentary's Third Council story, Cousins argues, draws its force from the double meaning. The present-day Theravāda is therefore **one branch** of the Vibhajjavādins, not the whole group. That matters below, because another branch reappears as an opponent.

### 2.3 · The northern definition `[cross-tradition]`

The Sarvāstivāda's own systematization, as reported and criticized by Vasubandhu, defines the two camps sharply (*Abhidharmakośabhāṣya* ad V.25, Pradhan ed., p. 296, ll. 4–6):

> *ye hi sarvam astīti vadanti atītam anāgataṃ pratyutpannaṃ ca te sarvāstivādāḥ / ye tu kecid asti yat pratyutpannam adattaphalaṃ cātītaṃ karma kiñcin nāsti yad dattaphalam atītam anāgataṃ ceti vibhajya vadanti te vibhajyavādinaḥ /*
>
> Those who say that *all exists*, past, future and present, are Sarvāstivādins. Those who, *distinguishing* (*vibhajya*), say that something exists (the present, and past action that has not given its fruit) and something does not (past action that has given its fruit, and the future) are Vibhajyavādins.

The *Kośa*'s Vibhajyavādin, then, holds **present plus some past, and no future**. The name is glossed from the method: they *speak distinguishing*. (Dowker cites Stcherbatsky p. 43 for the Vibhajavādin view. That page could not be consulted on the date of writing; the *Kośa* passage above is quoted from the primary text.)

## 3 · The Earliest Pāli Record: Four Debates on Time

The *Kathāvatthu* is a book of debates. The Theravādin (*sakavādī*) cross-examines an opponent (*paravādī*) by paired questions until the opponent's thesis yields a consequence he must reject. Four early debates concern time. The *Kathāvatthu* names no opponents; the attributions below are the commentary's. Paragraph numbers follow the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana edition, whose chapter division differs from the Pali Text Society's; the § numbers are unambiguous.

| CST § | Debate | Opponent's thesis | Commentary's attribution |
|---|---|---|---|
| 282 | *Sabbamatthītikathā* | *sabbam atthi*: all exists | *"seyyathāpi etarahi sabbamatthivādānaṃ"* (the all-exists school, i.e. the Sarvāstivāda) |
| 297 | *Atītakkhandhādikathā* | past and future things are aggregates, bases and elements, and therefore exist | the paravādin who "wants past and future to exist because they do not give up being aggregates" |
| 299–300 | *Ekaccaṃ atthītikathā* | *some* of the past exists (action whose result has not ripened), and some of the future (what is bound to arise) | *"seyyathāpi kassapikā"* (the Kassapikas, one of Cousins's four Vibhajjavādin branches) |
| 304 | *Hevatthikathā* | things exist *in one way* and not in another: the past exists *as past* | *"seyyathāpi etarahi … andhakānaṃ"* (the Andhakas) |

The refutations turn on one formula, applied to each thesis in turn:

> *Atītaṃ atthīti? Āmantā. Nanu atītaṃ niruddhaṃ vigataṃ vipariṇataṃ atthaṅgataṃ abbhatthaṅgatanti?* (§283)
>
> "Does the past exist?" — "Yes." — "But has the past not ceased, departed, changed, set, gone down?"

At §297 the Theravādin grants that past things are *classified* as aggregates, but denies that they *exist*. The commentary grounds this in the *Niruttipatha Sutta* (SN 22.62): what has passed is designated *"was"*, never *"is"* or *"will be"*. That sutta, in the Nikāyas themselves, is the oldest scriptural anchor of the view.

At §299 the Kassapika position is broken apart. If some of the past exists, then some of the past has ceased and some has not, and the part that has not ceased is not past. The same dilemma is run for the future at §300.

The position that remains, the Mahāvihāra's own, is **presentism**: only present phenomena exist. Past and future are what *was* and what *will be*.

```
  all four)                                                            says his model is NOT

  † the Sarvāstivāda ≈ eternalism mapping is published (Miller 2017); cited, not claimed.
```

Sorkin's growth is not presentism. In the sequential-growth models, what has been born **stays**: the causal set at any stage contains its whole past and lacks only its future. Sorkin's own words set the view against a present-only doctrine. Most arguments for the block universe, he writes, *"presuppose that the sole alternative to the 'block universe' is a doctrine that identifies reality with a three-dimensional instant"*. He also denies that the process has any single "now" spreading over it. Wüthrich and Callender (2017) examine what kind of becoming this is, and conclude that a novel notion of becoming is compatible with causal sets. Nothing in that literature reads the model as presentist.

**On the Buddhist map, Sorkin's accretion therefore sits in the *Kośa*'s Vibhajyavādin row: present plus a past that persists, and no future.** Sorkin's footnote is accurate about the school the northern sources describe. It is not accurate about the school that uses the name today.

## 5 · The Answer to Dowker

Dowker asked for "the Vibhajavadin view of time … by examining the earliest existing records." The records give a three-part answer.

1. **There is no single Vibhajjavādin view of time.** The name covered a family of schools (Cousins). In the northern sources it names a *present-plus-unripened-past* view. The Pāli branch's own debate book **refutes that view for the past** (§299), under the Kassapika name.
2. **The view nearest causal-set accretion is the one the Pāli Vibhajjavādin record argues against.** Sorkin's instinct matched a real Buddhist position; the position is the *Kośa*'s Vibhajyavāda and the Pāli Kassapika's past-half, not the Theravāda's.
3. **The Theravāda's own view is presentism**, grounded in the *Niruttipatha Sutta* and defended in the *Kathāvatthu* by a single reductio: whatever is past has ceased, and what has ceased does not exist.

This is a historical answer about what the records say. It takes no position on which view of time is true.

## 6 · The Real Point of Contact: Influence Without Existence

If the Theravāda is not a growing block, is there anything in it for a causal-set physicist? There is one thing, and it is narrower than the name suggested.

A presentist owes an account of how the past can matter if it does not exist. The Pāli Abhidhamma gives one. Its analysis of conditioning is the *Paṭṭhāna*, summarized in *Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha* ch. 8. The *Abhidhammatthavibhāvinī-ṭīkā*, Sumaṅgala's sub-commentary on the *Saṅgaha* (c. 12th century, a later layer than the *Saṅgaha* itself), makes explicit the temporal reach each kind of condition has. In the CST text, its comment on ch. 8, §37:

- **Five conditions reach only the immediate past:** proximity, contiguity, repetition, absence and disappearance (*anantara, samanantara, āsevana, natthi, vigata*). They carry the moment that has just ceased into the next.
- **The kamma condition reaches past or present** (*dvikālika*): an action long gone conditions its result now, across many moments (*nānākkhaṇika*). The past act does not exist when its result arises. What persists is the conditioning relation, not the act.
- **Three conditions reach all three times** (*tikālika*): the object, predominance and decisive-support conditions. So the future also conditions, as an object of thought or intention.

The *Kathāvatthu* adds the epistemological half of Dowker's question. At IX.6 (§559) an opponent holds that a consciousness whose object is past has no object, since the past does not exist. The Theravādin refutes this: a mind can turn toward the past, attend to it and aspire concerning it, so a past object is a genuine object even though it does not exist. **Perception and memory reach what is not.**

So the Theravāda's answer to "how does the past act?" is **influence without existence**: the past conditions the present, and is cognized by it, without being real now. A causal-set physicist might recognize a structural cousin here. In causal sets the order relation, not a background time, carries the physics. ⚠️ **But the analogy has a hard limit, and it must be stated.** In a causal set the order holds *between elements that remain in the set*; the past is kept. The Theravāda keeps the relation's effects and discards its past relatum. A presentist causal set would be a different ontology from Sorkin's, and whether one is coherent is a question for physics, not for this paper.

## 7 · The Strongest Objection, Carried

**Objection.** "Presentism is a Western category, and the Abhidhamma is a phenomenology of experience. It is not a metaphysics of spacetime. Mapping *atthi* ('exists') onto the ontology of a causal set compares two different questions."

**What survives it.** Much of the objection stands, and the paper does not try to answer it away. The *Kathāvatthu*'s *atthi* is about dhammas, momentary mental and material phenomena, not about spacetime points. Its debates are about what may be *said* to exist in a doctrinal register. And the Abhidhamma's momentariness has no metric: its moments carry no length, and its "present" is not a global simultaneity slice, which causal sets deny in any case. What survives is historical and exact: which positions on the existence of the past and the future were taken, by whom, and which the Pāli tradition rejected. Sorkin's footnote made a historical claim, and a historical claim can be checked against records. That is all this paper does with the physics.

**The counter-argument to this paper's own strongest claim.** Our strongest claim is that the Pāli Vibhajjavāda is Sorkin's foil. Against it: Cousins's group-sense of the name means Sorkin may simply have meant the *group*, in which the Kassapiyas are members. His footnote would then be *right* about a Vibhajjavādin branch. Nothing in this paper refutes that reading. We claim only that the branch that preserved the Pāli canon, and uses the name today, holds the opposite view.

## 8 · What Would Falsify These Findings

- **A primary Theravāda source that affirms the existence of unripened past kamma** (as opposed to its conditioning force) would undo §3's reading of the Mahāvihāra as presentist.
- **A reading of Sorkin's sequential growth in which past elements cease to exist**, rather than remain in the set, would move his view toward presentism and undo §4's placement. His own texts, read on the date of writing, say the opposite.
- **A reliable attribution of the §299 thesis to a school other than the Kassapikas**, or evidence that the *Kośa*'s Vibhajyavādin denoted the Mahāvihāra specifically, would change §5.1.
- **Stcherbatsky's p. 43**, once consulted, might attribute a different view to the Vibhajyavādins than the *Kośa* does. If so, Dowker's pointer and the *Kośa* diverge, and §2.3 must say so.

## 9 · Stake, and What Was and Was Not Done

**The authors' stake.** The authors write from within the Theravāda, and the institution publishing this paper grounds its values in the Pāli canon and has ruled Theravāda readings over Sarvāstivāda ones on its own questions. That creates a pull toward finding one's tradition anticipated by frontier physics. **This paper's first draft of the thesis made exactly that error.** It read the Pāli presentism as Sorkin's "becoming", on his side. An adversarial review, run before any prose was written, found that Sorkin's growth keeps the past, and the thesis was reversed. The reversal is the paper.

**Done:**
- The four time debates of the *Kathāvatthu* and their commentarial attributions read in the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana text;
- the *Kośa*'s definition read in Pradhan's Sanskrit;
- Sorkin 2007 and Dowker 2022/2023 read in full;
- the temporal classification of the conditions read in the CST text of the *Abhidhammatthavibhāvinī-ṭīkā* (a sub-commentary, not the *Saṅgaha* itself);
- the census run as described.

**Not done:**
- The Sarvāstivāda *Vibhāṣā* and the Chinese Āgama parallels (where the Kāśyapīya view is also reported) were not consulted.
- Stcherbatsky p. 43 could not be read (the archive was unavailable on the date of writing).
- Cousins was read in English; the Mahāvihāra's lineage texts (the *Dīpavaṃsa*) were read only as quoted by the commentary.
- The physics of a presentist causal set was not attempted.

## 10 · Honest Limits

- **The *Kathāvatthu*'s date is traditional.** It is ascribed to the Third Council (3rd century BCE), and much of the book is plausibly later. The commentary that supplies the self-designation and the opponents' names is c. 5th century CE. "Earliest existing records" here means the earliest the Pāli tradition preserves, not the earliest that existed.
- **The attributions are the commentary's.** The *Kathāvatthu* names no opponents. A later author's attribution is evidence about the tradition's self-understanding, not a contemporaneous report.
- **The physics mapping is a mapping of positions on existence.** It does not claim that any Buddhist school anticipated a theory of quantum gravity, and it makes no claim about which theory of time is correct.
- **One edition.** Paragraph numbers are the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana's; the Pali Text Society's chapter numbering differs. A reader should locate passages by § number and title.
- **The census was quick and English-only.** The field of Buddhism-and-physics comparison is large and poorly indexed. Absence from our search is weak evidence.

## 11 · Close

Sorkin heard, in a school name, an echo of the time his models grow. The echo is real. It comes from one branch of a family of schools, the one the northern sources describe. The branch that kept the Pāli canon answered that view in its oldest debate book, with a question it asks of every past thing: *has it not ceased?* The answer to Dowker's invitation is that the earliest records disagree with each other, in exactly the place where the physics of becoming is still open.
