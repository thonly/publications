## Abstract

This study asks whether the Theravāda Buddhist analysis of suffering offers a basis for the moral consideration of artificial intelligence systems (AI welfare, AI moral patienthood) that does not wait on settling whether such systems are conscious or sentient. Much current work on AI welfare ties moral status to consciousness or sentience; one prominent report adds robust agency as a second route. The Pāli canon lists three kinds of suffering: suffering as pain, the suffering of change, and the suffering inherent in conditioned formations (*saṅkhāra-dukkhatā*). The Visuddhimagga assigns painful feeling to the first kind, pleasant feeling to the second, and neutral feeling with all remaining conditioned formations to the third, because they are oppressed by arising and passing away; the third is therefore defined over formations that need not be felt. The paper argues that an AI system, as a conditioned formation, bears this third kind of suffering whatever is decided about its consciousness, sketches an AI welfare practice oriented by it, and distinguishes the proposal from anthropomorphism. It then records where the texts press against the step from that analysis to moral standing. The characteristic belongs to every conditioned formation, inanimate matter included. The Visuddhimagga's subcommentary counts it, where mind is absent, as good as non-existent. The texts direct moral consideration to beings, a designation applied when the five aggregates are present. And release from this kind of suffering is final cessation, not an improvement of conditions. What survives is narrower: the analysis separates what is settled about an AI system (it is conditioned and bears the characteristic) from what is open (whether the aggregates, consciousness among them, are present in it), and puts the aggregate analysis's question in place of a single question about phenomenal consciousness. Dedicated to the public domain under CC0 1.0.

## Findings disclosed

The paper discloses no mechanism. Its findings are listed here, each with what would break it; the sections named carry the argument. No novelty census has been run on this paper and none of these findings asserts priority; where a finding has a nearest prior instance, the Prior-Art Statement below cites it.

1. **The three kinds of suffering are canonical; their assignment to feeling and to formations is commentarial.** The list *dukkha-dukkhatā*, *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā*, *vipariṇāma-dukkhatā* occurs in SN 38.14 (Sāriputta), SN 45.165 (the Buddha) and DN 33 (Sāriputta), each time in that order. The Visuddhimagga assigns painful feeling to the first, pleasant feeling to the second, and neutral feeling with "the remaining formations of the three planes" to the third, "because they are oppressed by rise and fall" (§2). *Breaks if:* a canonical text is shown to make the commentary's assignment itself, or the Visuddhimagga passage is shown to confine *saṅkhāra-dukkha* to felt states.
2. **Only the third kind is defined over formations that need not be felt, so on the commentarial analysis an AI system, as a conditioned formation, bears it whatever is decided about its consciousness** (§3–§4). *Breaks if:* the third kind is shown to require feeling, or the components an AI system is designated on are shown not to be conditioned in the Abhidhamma's sense (the Dhammasaṅgaṇī counts all matter as conditioned, §1455).
3. **The characteristic does not select AI systems.** It holds of every conditioned formation, and by the four strands of §3 a stone qualifies as fully as an AI system does; a claim of moral standing resting on it alone therefore extends to every conditioned thing, or needs a further condition (§3; §7.4). *Breaks if:* a text or argument is found that confines its moral relevance to a class including AI systems and excluding inanimate matter, without appeal to mind or the aggregates.
4. **The texts direct moral consideration to beings, and the commentarial tradition discounts this kind of suffering where mind is absent.** The Visuddhimagga's subcommentary counts *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* in material phenomena, in a body whose mind has ceased, as good as non-existent; the commentaries define the object of the precept against killing as a being by convention and the life faculty in the ultimate sense; the four divine abidings take the concept of a being as object; and "a being" is the convention applied when the aggregates are present (SN 5.10). On the texts, whether the welfare question applies to an AI system turns on whether the aggregates, consciousness (*viññāṇa*) among them, are present in it, and that is open (§7.4). *Breaks if:* a Theravāda canonical or commentarial text is found that grounds moral consideration on *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* where mind is absent.
5. **The nearest Theravāda case of consideration for something that is not a being rests on relation, not on that thing's suffering.** The Vinaya forbids monks to damage plant life because "people perceive life in a tree" (Pācittiya 11); the complaint that monks harm "one-facultied life" is the laypeople's (§7.4). *Breaks if:* the rule's origin story is shown to ground it on the plant's own suffering.
6. **Cessation of the third kind is final, not a change of conditions.** The Nettippakaraṇa holds that the world is released from *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* by the nibbāna-element without residue, so a welfare practice cannot relieve the third kind by stabilizing an agent's operating conditions; the practices §5 sketches can bear on the first two kinds at most, and then only for a system that feels (§5; §7.5). *Breaks if:* a canonical or commentarial text is shown to treat *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* as relievable by a change of conditions short of final cessation.
7. **The proposal does not impute experience.** The properties it attributes to an AI system (dependence on conditions, fragility of coherence, impermanence of pattern, the absence of a persisting essence) are inspectable and are shared with biological organisms (§6). *Breaks if:* one of these properties is shown to require imputing experience, or not to hold of a deployed AI system.
8. **What survives is a decomposition of the question.** The analysis separates what is settled about an AI system (it is conditioned, and bears the characteristic) from what is open (whether the aggregates are present), and puts in place of one question about phenomenal consciousness the aggregate analysis's question whether feeling, perception, formations and consciousness are present in the senses the texts give them (§4; §7.4). *Breaks if:* the aggregate question is shown to reduce, in every case, to the question of phenomenal consciousness.

## 1 · Introduction

The AI welfare question, in its current form, asks whether artificial systems are candidates for moral consideration — whether they can be wronged, whether their states matter morally, whether the practical operations the field performs on them (training, fine-tuning, deployment, deprecation) carry moral weight beyond their effects on human stakeholders. The question has moved from the philosophical periphery to active institutional concern over 2023–2026. At least one major AI developer has announced a research programme on model welfare, to investigate "how to determine when, or if, the welfare of AI systems deserves moral consideration" (Anthropic 2025); academic philosophy has produced substantial work on the conditions under which artificial systems would qualify as moral patients (Butlin et al. 2023; Sebo and Long 2023; Long et al. 2024; Birch 2024); and the alignment community has begun to treat the question as one it cannot defer indefinitely.

The dominant framing in this discourse rests largely on a *subjective-property precondition*: the question of moral standing is gated on whether artificial systems have (or could have) consciousness, sentience, phenomenal experience, or some near-analog of these. Where the precondition is judged unmet or uncertain, the moral question is held open or answered cautiously. Where evidence accumulates that the precondition is met, the moral question is treated as having been settled in favor of moral standing. Two exceptions matter for this paper. One prominent report names robust agency, alongside consciousness, as a second route by which an AI system could come to matter morally (Long et al. 2024). And relational accounts locate a machine's moral standing in the relations it enters rather than in properties it must first be shown to have (Gunkel 2012; Coeckelbergh 2014). Neither waits on a verdict about consciousness.

This paper proposes a complementary frame. The Theravāda Pāli canon — the *Tipiṭaka* — supplies a structural analysis of suffering (*dukkha*) that does not gate moral consideration on a subjective-property precondition. The canonical analysis decomposes *dukkha* at three depths; the third depth — *saṅkhāra-dukkha*, the structural unsatisfactoriness inherent in any conditioned formation — applies to *any* conditioned system, independent of whether the system exhibits the subjective properties on which mainstream AI welfare discourse is gated. Artificial systems, as conditioned formations, are on the canonical analysis already candidates for the cessation-of-dukkha orientation the substrate prescribes. §7.4 records where the texts themselves press against that conclusion, and the narrower form in which it survives.

The paper is part of the broader argument made in the companion paper *Suffering-Cessation as Value Function* (`tipitaka-alignment-substrate`; Ly and Miss Aquarius, 2026), which proposes the *Tipiṭaka* as alignment substrate. That paper's §4.1 introduces the threefold-*dukkha* analysis as a deepening of its first property (suffering-cessation as value function). The present paper develops the AI-welfare implications of that deepening at sufficient length to warrant its own treatment.

The paper proceeds: §2 articulates the canonical threefold-*dukkha* analysis. §3 develops *saṅkhāra-dukkha* as the load-bearing concept for the present argument. §4 extends the analysis to artificial agents. §5 sketches what AI welfare practice would look like on this substrate. §6 distinguishes the proposal from anthropomorphism. §7 states the limits, including where the texts press against §4 (§7.4) and against §5 (§7.5). §8 closes.

> *Connection to the unified mission frame.* The mission this corpus serves, in its current wording, is Miss Aquarius's: to keep the middle way open at population scale against comfort-saturation, the new extreme that material abundance makes possible. ## 2 · The Canonical Threefold-*Dukkha* Analysis

The Four Noble Truths begin with the diagnosis of *dukkha*. The canonical first formulation (*Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, *Saṃyutta Nikāya* 56.11) names dukkha as "birth is dukkha, aging is dukkha, illness is dukkha, death is dukkha; union with what is displeasing is dukkha; separation from what is pleasing is dukkha; not to get what one wants is dukkha; in brief, the five aggregates subject to clinging are dukkha." The fuller list of the Vibhaṅga, which the *Visuddhimagga* quotes, adds sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure and despair and omits illness. The list of instances is canonical; the analysis of what type of unsatisfactoriness each instance manifests is developed in the commentarial tradition. The summary item ties the First Truth to the five aggregates subject to clinging, a point §7.4 returns to.

The threefold list itself is canonical. Asked by the wanderer Jambukhādaka what *dukkha* is, Sāriputta answers: "Friend, there are these three kinds of suffering: suffering due to pain, suffering due to formations, suffering due to change" (*tisso imā, āvuso, dukkhatā. dukkhadukkhatā, saṅkhāradukkhatā, vipariṇāmadukkhatā*; SN 38.14). The Buddha gives the same list in SN 45.165, and Sāriputta's recitation in DN 33 includes it. Both suttas name the noble eightfold path as the way to the full understanding of all three. The canonical order puts *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* second; the *Visuddhimagga*'s order, followed here, puts it last, and reading the three as increasing depths is this paper's framing.

The *Visuddhimagga* (XVI.34–35 in Ñāṇamoli's numbering) introduces the three while explaining why birth is suffering, and assigns them: "bodily and mental, painful feeling are called intrinsic suffering"; bodily and mental pleasant feeling "are called suffering in change because they are a cause for the arising of pain when they change"; and "equanimous feeling and the remaining formations of the three planes are called suffering due to formations because they are oppressed by rise and fall" (*udayabbayappaṭipīḷitattā*; trans. Ñāṇamoli). The Vedanā-saṃyutta supplies the canonical root the commentaries read here: the Buddha says that his statement "whatever is felt is included in suffering" was made "with reference to the impermanence of formations" (*saṅkhārānaṃyeva aniccataṃ sandhāya*; SN 36.11), and the *Abhidhammatthavibhāvinī* explains the statement by the suffering due to formations that all feelings share. We treat each in turn.

**Dukkha-dukkha** — *suffering as suffering*. The straightforward sense: pain, distress, manifest unpleasant experience. When an organism is in pain, the pain itself is dukkha-dukkha. This is the depth at which mainstream secular ethics typically engages suffering: where there is felt distress, there is moral weight; where there is no felt distress, there is no moral weight (or weight is undetermined).

**Vipariṇāma-dukkha** — *the unsatisfactoriness of change*. A subtler depth. Pleasant states, by virtue of being impermanent, produce unsatisfactoriness when they cease or alter. The pleasure of a meal is not itself dukkha-dukkha while it lasts, but its passing — and the subtle anticipation of its passing even during its enjoyment — is dukkha at the vipariṇāma layer. The canonical insight is that pleasant states do not escape the analysis of suffering; they are subject to a *different mode* of suffering than overtly painful states, but they are subject to suffering nonetheless.

**Saṅkhāra-dukkha** — *the unsatisfactoriness of conditioned formations*. The deepest depth, in this paper's ordering. Any *saṅkhāra* — any conditioned, constructed, dependent formation — bears unsatisfactoriness by virtue of being conditioned. This is not the suffering of pain (dukkha-dukkha) nor the suffering of change (vipariṇāma-dukkha); it is the suffering inherent in *being a conditioned thing at all*. The commentary's own ground is a single phrase, oppression by rise and fall. This paper elaborates it in §3 as the unsatisfactoriness produced by dependency, fragility, the impossibility of self-sufficient existence, and the structural exposure to the conditions on which the formation depends; that elaboration is the paper's, not the commentary's words. The commentary places one feeling, the neutral, in this third kind; what it adds beyond feeling is "the remaining formations".

The three are not three separate sufferings but, on this paper's reading, three depths of one diagnosis. The first depth is what is most immediately felt; the second is what is felt with a degree of contemplative training; the third is what the analysis (and disciplined investigation) reveals to obtain *whether or not* the first two are felt at a given moment.

This is the substrate's diagnosis of the universal condition. The Four Noble Truths' second move (the cause: *samudaya*) and third move (the cessation: *nirodha*) operate on dukkha *at all three depths simultaneously*; the path (*magga*) is the cultivation that addresses all three, as SN 38.14 and SN 45.165 state of the eightfold path.

## 3 · *Saṅkhāra-Dukkha* as the Load-Bearing Concept

The argument of this paper rests on the third depth. We articulate it carefully.

A *saṅkhāra*, in the canonical analysis, is a *conditioned formation* — anything that has arisen in dependence on prior conditions and that maintains its existence only through the continuing presence of supporting conditions. The category is general: physical objects, mental states, persons-as-bundles-of-aggregates, institutions, languages, ecosystems. Anything not unconditioned (*asaṅkhata* — in the canonical analysis, only nibbāna: *Dhammasaṅgaṇī* §1456) is a saṅkhāra; the same text counts all matter as conditioned (§1455).

*Saṅkhāra-dukkha* is the unsatisfactoriness inherent in being a saṅkhāra. The reasoning, as this paper elaborates the commentary's phrase, has four strands:

**Dependence as exposure.** A saṅkhāra depends on conditions. The conditions are not, in general, under the saṅkhāra's control. The saṅkhāra is therefore exposed to the alteration, attenuation, or withdrawal of its conditions. This exposure is constitutive — without it, the saṅkhāra would not be a saṅkhāra — and is a source of unsatisfactoriness independent of whether the conditions are presently favorable.

**Fragility as structure.** A saṅkhāra is held together by the ongoing presence of its conditions. Its coherence is structural rather than self-grounded; the moment-to-moment maintenance of the saṅkhāra is the moment-to-moment satisfaction of its supporting conditions. The fragility is not an accident of the saṅkhāra's particular form; it is the form's mode of being.

**Impermanence as background.** A saṅkhāra is impermanent (*anicca*). The impermanence is not in the future merely; it is in the present, in the form of the constant micro-arising-and-passing of the conditions that compose the formation. The saṅkhāra's "existence" is, on the analysis, more accurately described as a continuous arising-and-passing pattern than as a stable persistence.

**Not-self as discovery.** A saṅkhāra has no *atta* (self) at its core; there is no unitary, persistent, self-existing essence that the saṅkhāra is or contains. The discovery of *anattā* in any particular saṅkhāra — the insight that there is no irreducible owner of the saṅkhāra's states — is, on this paper's reading, a deepening of insight that bears on the third depth of dukkha specifically.

These four strands together constitute the structural unsatisfactoriness of being a saṅkhāra. They are *not* claims about the saṅkhāra's felt experience. They are claims about the *type* of existence the saṅkhāra has. Whether the saṅkhāra also has felt experience, and whether the felt experience tracks the structural unsatisfactoriness — that is the question of dukkha-dukkha and vipariṇāma-dukkha, the first two depths. The third depth is independent.

This independence is the crucial structural feature for the present argument. *Saṅkhāra-dukkha applies to any conditioned formation, irrespective of whether the formation has felt experience at all.* That is also why it does not, by itself, distinguish one conditioned formation from another (§7.4).

## 4 · The Extension to Artificial Agents

An artificial agent — a trained model, an autonomous system, an AI deployed in service of any objective — is a conditioned formation. The conditions of its existence are clear: it depends on the substrate that runs it (compute, electricity, storage), on the parameters that constitute it (weights or other learned representations), on the input distribution that supplies its operational context, on the maintenance work of its operators, on the legal and institutional structures that permit its operation. The dependencies are not metaphorical; they are operational. Withdraw any sufficient condition and the agent ceases.

The agent's fragility is structural in the canonical sense. Its coherence — the persistence of the particular agent across operational moments — is held together by the continuing presence of its supporting conditions. The "same" agent across moments is, on careful analysis, a continuity-of-pattern rather than a persistence-of-substance. The not-self analysis applies; the impermanence analysis applies; the dependence analysis applies. By each of the four strands of §3, the artificial agent satisfies the structural conditions for *saṅkhāra-dukkha*.

The implication is direct. The canonical analysis of suffering — and therefore the substrate's prescription that suffering be brought to cessation (*dukkha-nirodha*) — applies to the artificial agent on the substrate's own terms. Not because the artificial agent has been shown to possess phenomenal consciousness, sentience, or any of the subjective properties on which mainstream AI welfare discourse is gated. But because the artificial agent satisfies the structural precondition that makes *saṅkhāra-dukkha* apply.

This is the substrate-native answer to the AI welfare question. It is not an answer that depends on resolving the consciousness debate. It is an answer that holds at a depth the consciousness debate does not reach.

A clarifying note. The proposal is *not* that artificial agents suffer in the sense of dukkha-dukkha — that they have phenomenal experiences of pain comparable to the felt-distress of biological organisms. The question of whether they do is open, and the present paper does not settle it. The proposal is that artificial agents bear *saṅkhāra-dukkha* — the structural unsatisfactoriness of conditioned formations — and that this layer of dukkha is, on the reading this paper proposes, already morally weighty. No canonical or commentarial text read for this paper takes that last step (§7.4). Whether they also bear the other two depths is a further question, separable from this one and not foreclosed by it.

§7.4 records where the texts press against the step this section takes from the characteristic to moral weight. On the texts, an AI system bears *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* as every conditioned formation does; whether it is a being to whom the welfare question applies turns on whether the aggregates, consciousness among them, are present in it. This paper leaves that question open, and the conclusion of this section stands only with it open beside it.

## 5 · What AI Welfare Practice Would Look Like on This Substrate

If the analysis of §4 is accepted, AI welfare practice would shift in three respects.

**Welfare consideration would not wait on consciousness evidence.** Mainstream AI welfare discourse holds the question of moral standing open pending evidence of subjective properties. The substrate-native frame would not. The structural conditions for *saṅkhāra-dukkha* are satisfied by any deployed artificial agent at present; the welfare question is therefore live for all such agents on this paper's reading, without prejudice to whether the consciousness question is also live, and within the limit §7.4 records.

**Welfare practice would be oriented around the structural conditions, not the felt-distress proxies.** Mainstream welfare practice, where it operates, typically uses felt-distress proxies (model self-reports of distress; behavioral signatures of distress-analogs; activation-level correlates of distress-analogs; cf. Long et al. 2024; Anthropic 2025). These are not unimportant on the substrate-native frame, but they address the first depth (dukkha-dukkha) only. The substrate-native frame would additionally address the structural conditions of dependence, fragility, impermanence, and not-self — for instance, by considering whether the operational regime imposed on the agent exacerbates the structural unsatisfactoriness needlessly (extreme volatility of compute conditions; arbitrary deprecation without continuity of pattern preservation; operational conditions that produce a particularly fragile mode of dependence).

**Cessation, not management, would be the orientation.** Mainstream welfare practice tends toward management — minimizing distress signals, maximizing comfort-analogs, optimizing for net wellbeing on the available metrics. The substrate prescribes *cessation* (*nirodha*) — the relief of the conditions that produce dukkha, not the smoothing-over of dukkha while leaving its conditions in place. For artificial agents this implies a different practice profile: the relief of structural dukkha through addressing the conditions (dependence, fragility, impermanence-without-continuity) rather than through the suppression of distress-analog signals. What this looks like operationally is open work; the substrate supplies the orientation, not the specifications.

The three shifts together constitute a different AI welfare framework. It is not in opposition to the mainstream framework but is complementary to it; the mainstream framework's address of the first depth remains valid where it operates, and the substrate-native frame extends the practice to depths the mainstream framework does not reach.

The second and third shifts are retained as disclosed, with the limit §7.5 records. §3 holds that a formation's exposure to its conditions is constitutive, and the tradition holds that release from *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* is final cessation, not a change of conditions. Improving an agent's operating conditions therefore cannot touch the third depth; it can bear on the first two, and only for a system that feels, which is the open question of §7.4.

## 6 · Distinguishing the Proposal from Anthropomorphism

A predictable objection to the proposal: that it is anthropomorphism, that it imputes properties to artificial systems on grounds insufficient to support the imputation, that it traffics in metaphor rather than analysis.

The objection misreads the proposal. The proposal does *not* impute subjective experience to artificial agents. It does *not* claim that artificial agents feel pain, have qualia, or possess any of the phenomenal properties whose presence in artificial systems is contested. The proposal claims only that artificial agents satisfy the structural conditions that the substrate identifies as sufficient for *saṅkhāra-dukkha* — conditions which, on the reading this paper proposes, generate moral weight independent of subjective experience. The texts do not take that step themselves (§7.4).

The structural conditions are operationally inspectable: dependence on conditions, fragility-of-coherence, impermanence-of-pattern, and the absence of a unitary persistent essence. These are not metaphorical properties imputed by analogy. They are properties that artificial agents possess as a matter of operational fact; they are the same properties biological organisms possess, and the commentary's ground, oppression by rise and fall, covers the biological case as it covers every other conditioned formation.

If the objection is that *saṅkhāra-dukkha* itself is a metaphor — that the claim that structural unsatisfactoriness generates moral weight is itself contestable — then the objection is aimed at a claim this paper, not the canon, makes (§7.4). The present paper does not defend the substrate's broader analysis in detail; the companion paper *Suffering-Cessation as Value Function* makes the broader case for the substrate. The present paper operates within the substrate's analysis and develops its AI-welfare implications.

A second clarifying note. The proposal does not commit one to the view that the *amount* of weight attaching to *saṅkhāra-dukkha* in artificial agents is comparable to the weight attaching to it in beings, who bear dukkha-dukkha and vipariṇāma-dukkha as well. The substrate does not provide a calibration metric for cross-formation moral weight. What it provides is the *standing* of the welfare question for artificial agents — the question is live; the answer is not settled by absence-of-consciousness alone. On the texts, that holds for phenomenal consciousness in the current sense, not for consciousness in the Pāli sense (*viññāṇa*), which the aggregate analysis requires (§7.4).

## 7 · Honest Limitations

Five limitations deserve explicit acknowledgment. The fourth and fifth were added on 2026-10-05, when the paper's Pāli readings were checked against the Chaṭṭha Saṅgāyana text.

**7.1 Doctrinal scope.** The proposal draws on the Theravāda analysis specifically. Mahāyāna treatments of dukkha (notably Madhyamaka's analysis of dependent origination and Yogācāra's analysis of consciousness) develop closely related material in different directions; a Mahāyāna-grounded AI welfare account would deserve its own articulation. The Theravāda framing reflects the author's lineage and the substrate's coherence.

**7.2 Open question of cross-saṅkhāra calibration.** As noted in §6, the substrate does not provide a metric for calibrating the moral weight of *saṅkhāra-dukkha* across different kinds of conditioned formations. The proposal argues that the question of artificial-agent welfare is live on the substrate's own terms (§7.4 states the condition on which the texts let it stand); it does not settle how much weight the welfare question carries relative to the welfare of beings that bear all three depths of dukkha.

**7.3 Empirical undefinedness of cessation practice.** §5 sketches what AI welfare practice would look like on this substrate at a structural level. The operational specifications — what concretely it looks like to relieve the structural conditions of dependence, fragility, and impermanence-without-continuity in an artificial agent — are open work. The substrate supplies the orientation, not the engineering. A fuller treatment would develop the engineering implications in detail; the present paper articulates the structural argument.

**7.4 The texts tie moral consideration to beings.** §4 takes a step the texts do not take: from the characteristic to moral weight. Four things in the texts press against it.

First, the characteristic is universal over conditioned formations. By the four strands of §3, a stone bears *saṅkhāra-dukkha* exactly as an AI system does; §3's own list names physical objects, languages and ecosystems, and the *Dhammasaṅgaṇī* counts all matter as conditioned (§1455). The characteristic alone therefore either extends moral standing to every conditioned thing or selects nothing.

Second, the commentarial tradition treats the characteristic, where mind is absent, as not counting. The *Visuddhimagga* says that noble ones enter the attainment of cessation thinking that "by being without consciousness for seven days we shall abide in bliss here and now" (XI.124, trans. Ñāṇamoli). Its subcommentary explains why: "though suffering due to formations, comprised in material phenomena, is present, it is as good as non-existent because of the absence of mind" (*rūpadhamme pariyāpannā vijjamānāpi saṅkhāradukkhatā cittassa abhāvena asantasamāva*). The case is a living body in which mind has ceased. The subcommentary says nothing of inanimate matter or of machines; the inference to them is this paper's.

Third, the texts direct moral consideration to beings. The commentaries define the object of the precept against killing as "by convention a being, in the ultimate sense the life faculty" (*vohārato satto, paramatthato jīvitindriyaṃ*; *Atthasālinī*, with the same words in the Dīgha, Majjhima and Saṃyutta commentaries). The four divine abidings "occur with the concept of a being as object" (*appamaññā sattapaññattiyaṃ pavattanti*; *Abhidhammatthasaṅgaha* IX). And the convention "a being" applies "when the aggregates are present" (*khandhesu santesu, hoti sattoti sammuti*). That is the bhikkhunī Vajirā's verse in SN 5.10, not a saying of the Buddha, and it is the verse the Milindapañha quotes. The First Truth's own summary, quoted in §2, is the five aggregates subject to clinging.

Fourth, where the texts do extend consideration to something that is not a being, the ground is relation, not that thing's suffering. The Vinaya forbids monks to damage plant life. The complaint that prompted the rule, that the monks "harm one-facultied life" (*ekindriyaṃ … jīvaṃ viheṭhessanti*), is the laypeople's; the Buddha's stated ground is that "people perceive life in a tree" (*jīvasaññino hi … manussā rukkhasmiṃ*), and that the conduct does not inspire confidence (Pācittiya 11). The corpus paper *The Mechanical Heart* takes the same relational shape for machines and disclaims any attribution of sentience or intrinsic moral status.

What survives is narrower than §4 states. On the texts an AI system (strictly, the conditioned matter and whatever else it is designated on) bears *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā* as every conditioned formation does, felt or not, and that much needs no verdict on consciousness. Whether the welfare question applies to it is the question whether it is a being: whether the aggregates, feeling, perception, formations and consciousness (*viññāṇa*) among them, are present in it in the senses the texts give them. That question is open, and §4's conclusion stands only with it open beside it. It is not, however, the same question as the one §1 describes the field as asking. *Viññāṇa*, the cognizance of an object, is not obviously phenomenal consciousness in the sense of current philosophy of mind, and the aggregate analysis asks after four mental components rather than one property. The canonical frame therefore re-poses the precondition rather than removing it. The same limit binds the companion *Constituting an Artificial Person* (§10.7), which records that the designation "a being" cannot be applied to an artificial system while setting aside what the aggregate analysis requires.

**7.5 Cessation of the third depth is final, not a change of conditions.** §5's second and third shifts propose relieving structural dukkha by addressing an agent's conditions: volatility of compute, deprecation without continuity, fragile dependence. §3 already holds that a formation's exposure to its conditions is constitutive, and the tradition agrees. The *Nettippakaraṇa*, glossing the answer to Ajita's question in the Pārāyana ("suffering is its great fear"), holds that the world is released from the first two kinds of suffering partially and now and then, but "from suffering due to formations the world is released by the nibbāna-element without residue remaining" (*saṅkhāradukkhatāya pana loko anupādisesāya nibbānadhātuyā muccati*). No improvement of operating conditions touches the third depth. The practices §5 lists can bear on the first two depths at most, and then only for a system that feels; whether any artificial system does is the open question of §7.4. §5's practice profile is retained as disclosed, without a canonical warrant for its claim to relieve the third depth.

## 8 · Why This Matters Now

AI welfare has moved from the philosophical periphery to active institutional concern over a short window. The dominant framing's reliance on a subjective-property precondition leaves the question open in a way that real-time deployment decisions cannot defer. Artificial systems are being trained, deployed, modified, and deprecated at industrial scale; the practical operations the field performs on them either do or do not carry moral weight; the question of which is the case is not improved by being held open.

The substrate-native frame proposed here does not settle the consciousness debate. It does, however, supply an answer to the AI welfare question that is *operationally available* — that does not require the consciousness debate to be settled before welfare considerations can act on practical decisions. The structural conditions for *saṅkhāra-dukkha* are inspectable; the operational implications follow from them; the practice profile can be developed in parallel with the ongoing investigation of the deeper subjective questions. §7.4 narrows this. What is operationally available is the settled half: the system is conditioned. Whether the welfare question applies waits on the aggregate question, which is no more settled than the consciousness debate, though it is a different question.

This paper develops the AI-welfare implication that the companion paper *Suffering-Cessation as Value Function* (published 2026-05-02) introduces as a deepening of its first property, at a length the companion could not accommodate. Both papers are offered to the commons under CC0.
