## Preamble

The oldest description of almsgiving in the Pāli canon is an agricultural one. A verse at the head of the *Petavatthu* says that the worthy are like fields, the givers like farmers, the gift like seed, and that the fruit arises from these together [C]. Read plainly, the simile describes a working system and not an ornament: a field is something cultivated, a farmer is someone who acts, and a harvest depends on the condition of the ground. The tradition built that system out of renunciation. A monastic who holds no money, owns no stock and trades nothing becomes, in the tradition's own phrase, an unsurpassed field of merit for the world, and laypeople plant in that field by feeding it.

The same tradition also knew the system's logistics problem. A commentary on the monastic code records a village so far from the monastery that the monks did not want to walk there every day, and the villagers complained that they were being left out of merit [C]. The commentary's answer is administrative: monks in a nearer residence are asked to eat there on the days the others do not come, and the meal tickets are to be distributed every day. Two and a half thousand years later the complaint has a new form. Families who emigrated cannot reach the round at all; givers who carry no cash cannot give at the door; and the cash that does reach the bowl is the one thing the code forbids a monastic to accept.

This paper specifies an instrument for that problem. It is a paper ticket that a layperson can buy at a shop, carry, pass on and drop into a bowl, so that the gesture of giving stays in human hands at the round; and it is designed so that, unlike the restaurant ticket, the gift voucher and the banknote, it can never become money, because the ticket itself can release nothing. The food is released only when the vendor scans a credential that hangs from the monk's bowl, after the monastery's own appointed officer has assigned the meal by lot. The design rests on a reading of the monastic code's definition of money as a checklist, and on six canonical procedures, each of which carries one step of the flow. The canon is cited as lineage and as the specification the design must satisfy; the mechanism survives with every Pāli term removed (§13), and the tier labels — [C] canon, [ours] the authors' mapping — mark which is which.

## Abstract

This paper discloses a prepaid food-offering system for Buddhist monastic alms rounds in which a layperson buys a meal portion at a local food vendor, the portion is held at the vendor as the giver's property, and a paper ticket linked to it can be carried, passed on and dropped into a monk's alms bowl without ever functioning as money. The ticket carries an identifier, a machine-readable code, the words *for the Sangha* and the name of the one shop that sold it, and nothing else: no amount, no dish, no giver and no date. Tickets collected at the monastery are assigned to monks each morning by a verifiable lot whose roster is committed by an officer the monastic community appoints, so that every released gift reaches a monk through the community's own assignment, no giver chooses a monk, and nothing in the system grades one. The next morning the assigned monk stands at the vendor's door with the ticket and the bowl visible and does not approach; the vendor chooses to approach, scans a revocable credential that hangs from the bowl and is issued and revoked by the monastic community, and fills the bowl. Only that scan releases the food; the ticket routes the gift and signals the assignment, and then clears for reuse. Two clocks govern the gift: the purchased portion never expires, while the link from a portion to a ticket lapses at noon on the day after it was made, returning a known giver's portion to her holding and sending an anonymous portion to an opt-in householder drawn by lot, never the issuing vendor, who receives it as an instrument she can only give onward. The same object carries gifts from three sources, a local giver, a remote giver acting through a drawn local householder, and an institutional giver funding the vendor's capacity, and the householder at the bowl cannot tell them apart. The design is derived by using the monastic code's functional definition of money — *whatever passes in trade* — as a checklist the ticket must fail clause by clause. A prior-art census and an adversarial pass found every individual mechanism public and the composition not found in their aperture; the claims are scoped to the composition, the orphaned-gift lapse rule and the design-time use of the definition. A Vinaya commentary that counts restaurant tickets as money is engaged as the strongest objection, and the paper's limits record where the design meets monastic administrative rules, the alms-food-only practice and the trade rule.

**Keywords:** alms round, piṇḍapāta, Buddhist monastic food offering, meal ticket, prepaid meal, suspended meal, pay-it-forward meal, food voucher, gift certificate, non-monetary token, bearer token, recipient credential, revocable credential, co-presence release, random assignment, lottery allocation, verifiable lottery, salākabhatta, bhattuddesaka, kappiya, Vinaya, Nissaggiya Pācittiya 18, Nissaggiya Pācittiya 10, gift expiry, breakage, unclaimed gift, escheat alternative, remote giving, diaspora remittance, capacity funding.

## 1 · The problem: a token that must enter the bowl without becoming money

### 1.1 · Why money in the bowl is the problem

The monastic code forbids a monk to take, have taken for him, or consent to gold, silver or money held for him, and it defines the forbidden thing functionally. The rule's own word-commentary says that *rajata* ("silver") means the *kahāpaṇa*, the copper coin, the wooden coin, the lacquer coin, and whatever else passes in trade (*ye vohāraṃ gacchanti*) [C]. The list is the point. A wooden token is not silver; it is forbidden because it circulates. The category is defined by what a thing does, not by what it is made of, and a design that wants something to enter the bowl has to satisfy that definition, not a narrower one.

Cash is now a common sight in alms bowls in mainland Southeast Asia. The corpus's earlier answer to it, *The Bowl That Holds No Money*, was to keep every token out of the bowl: a credential tag hangs from the bowl's sling, a donor who wants to give money scans it, and the money goes to a lay steward's fund, so that the bowl receives food only. That paper's signage — *this bowl receives food only* — is part of what the monastic community is asked to endorse.

### 1.2 · Why a token is wanted anyway

Three groups of givers are not served by a bowl that receives food only and a phone that receives money.

- **The giver who cooks nothing.** A worker who passes the round on the way to work, or a grandmother who no longer cooks, has no food to give at six in the morning. She can buy food the day before, but the round is the moment of giving, and she wants to give at it.
- **The giver who carries no phone.** A scan-to-give credential serves people with phones and bank accounts. A paper ticket bought with cash at a familiar counter serves everyone else, and in the target region that is a large share of the people who give.
- **The giver who is not there.** Families who have emigrated send money home, and some of it is meant for the monastery. Sent as money, it either reaches the bowl as money or reaches a steward's fund, where it buys requisites but does not put food into a bowl at the round.

Each of these wants a *token*: something that can be bought ahead, carried, handed to another person and placed in the bowl. And every token that can be bought ahead and handed on is, on its face, exactly what the definition in §1.1 forbids.

### 1.3 · The design goal, stated as a negative

The goal is not a token that is *treated* as non-money by agreement. It is a token that **cannot do what money does**, so that no ruling, enforcer or good intention is needed to keep it out of the category. Stated as a negative:

> No person — giver, carrier, monk, vendor or institution — can use the ticket to obtain anything except one thing: a portion of food, already paid for by someone else, poured into the bowl of the one monk the community's lot assigned, by the vendor who holds it, in that monk's presence.

The rest of this paper is that sentence made mechanical. The ticket competes with cash on the *gesture* — something light, handed over at the round — and never on the *circulation*: it is designed so that it cannot circulate as money, and the design does not need it to.

### 2.3 · The strongest objection, found by the census

The census found something more important than prior art. A modern commentary on the money rule, written in the Burmese tradition and published on dhammadana.org, lists what is to be considered money as *"all precious metals, coins, bank notes, checks, credit cards, restaurant tickets or any other type of monetary means (all that which enables to buy something)."* It continues: *"However, telephone cards, stamps and transportation tickets do not belong to this category as these things do not enable one to do shopping."* The English page is a translation of a French original, in which *tickets restaurant* are the employer-issued meal vouchers accepted as payment at many restaurants and shops — an instrument that is generally accepted and carries a fixed value. The strict reading is not confined to that page: Bhikkhu Pesala, writing in the same tradition, says of the view that a credit card or check account is allowable that *"with careful reflection this is clearly no different to using money."*

That commentary is a direct counterexample to the naive form of this paper's thesis. A meal ticket is, on its face, a restaurant ticket; a monastic community that reads the rule this way would refuse it, and would be right to refuse any meal ticket that works like one. The commentary also states the test this paper's ticket is built to pass. The excluded tickets are excluded because they *enable no shopping*: a stamp moves a letter and a transport ticket moves a passenger, and neither can be turned into anything else. §8 shows, clause by clause, that the ticket disclosed here enables no purchase by anyone — not by the monk, not by the vendor and not by any layperson who holds it — because the ticket releases nothing. Whether a given community accepts that argument is that community's decision (§16).

Its estimated expiry, before any term adjustment, is around June 2035. It is cited here for what it discloses. Nothing in this paper reads its claims or characterizes any design's relation to them.

## 3 · The canonical frame

The design does not invent a monastic procedure. It operationalizes six that exist, each carrying one step of the flow, and one further text that explains why the assignment step matters. The canon is [C]; every mapping onto the design is [ours].

### 3.1 · Where the food waits: the householder's allowable place

The Mahāvagga allows four kinds of place in which food for monastics may be kept and cooked without the monks themselves storing it; the fourth is the *gahapati*, the householder's place [C] (Mahāvagga VI.33). The commentary explains it: a building that laypeople give, or their own houses, count as allowable because what is received from, stored by and kept overnight among the property of anyone other than a monk is allowable to a monk; and it adds that a building belonging to the community or to a monk is *not* a householder's place [C]. Ownership is the condition. Food kept on lay premises, as lay property, is not the monastery's stored food.

[ours] A B-Alms portion is kept at a lay vendor's shop and remains the property of the person who bought it until it reaches a bowl. It is never the monastery's and never the vendor's. That is the commentary's condition taken literally, and it is why the design refuses to let any portion become a balance held *for* the community (§4.3). The allowance itself governs *food* kept overnight; until the morning it is cooked, what the vendor holds is a giver's payment for an allowable thing, which is the Meṇḍaka case of §3.2. The two anchors divide the portion's life between them: the money side before cooking, the food side after.

### 3.2 · Donor, steward and monk: the robe-fund rule

Nissaggiya Pācittiya 10 sets out, step by step, what happens when a donor sends a fund for a monk's robe by messenger [C]. The monk refuses the fund in a fixed form: *we do not accept robe-funds; we accept robes, at the proper time, when allowable.* If asked, he points out a lay steward. The messenger instructs the steward and reports back: *the steward you pointed out has been instructed; let the venerable approach him at the proper time and he will clothe you.* The monk may then remind the steward two or three times; if that fails, he may stand silently in the steward's presence, up to six times, and no more. If the robe still does not come, the monk sends word to the donor: *the fund you sent does the monk no good; recover your own, so that your own is not lost.* The rule calls this *the proper course*.

[ours] The rule is the B-Alms flow, written about robes:

| NP 10 [C] | B-Alms [ours] |
|---|---|
| The refusal formula: *we do not accept the fund; we accept the robe* | The bowl's signage: it receives food only |
| A steward pointed out by the monk | A vendor designated by the monastery (§11) |
| The messenger's report: *he has been instructed; approach him at the proper time* | The ticket: a notice that something waits, not the thing itself |
| The monk stands silently, at most six times | The monk stands at the door and never approaches; the design is stricter, silent only — but see below: silent standing is itself a prompt |
| *Recover your own, so that your own is not lost* | The lapse returns the portion to the giver's holding (§9) — a weaker remedy than the canon's, because the holding is at the same vendor (§17) |

The commentary on the rule treats the silent standing as a prompt of half the weight of a spoken reminder (*ekā codanā diguṇaṃ ṭhānaṃ*, one reminder counts as two standings), so that a monk who only stands may stand up to twelve times [C]. The design's *never approaches* therefore does not mean *never prompts*: the monk at the door with a ticket is making a permitted, bounded prompt — one standing per assigned portion — of the kind the rule allows a monk to make of a steward who holds a fund for him.

The transposition from robes to food is ours, and is carried by the Meṇḍaka allowance [C] (Mahāvagga VI.34): where devout people place money with a lay steward saying *give the venerable whatever is allowable*, a monk may consent to the allowable thing that comes of it — and to no gold or silver, by any means.

### 3.3 · Who decides which monk: the meal officer

The Cullavagga allows a community to offer meals in several forms, including the meal by ticket (*salākabhatta*) [C] (Cullavagga VI.21). It then records the problem that created the office: a group of monks took the good meals for themselves and gave the worse ones to others. The remedy is an officer, the *bhattuddesaka*, appointed by the community's formal act, with five qualities: he does not act from desire, aversion, delusion or fear, and he knows what has and has not been assigned [C]. And when the new officers asked how meals should be assigned, the answer was to tie them to a ticket or a slip, heap them together, and assign [C].

The commentary describes the canonical ticket: a stick of heartwood, or a strip of bamboo, cane or palm leaf, inscribed *the ticket meal of So-and-so*; the tickets are put in a basket or a fold of cloth, heaped, shuffled over and over, and handed out by the officer in the order of the queue or, if there is none, from the senior seat [C].

[ours] This is the B-Alms link, in the canon. A portion is *tied to a ticket*; the tickets are *heaped* at the monastery; the officer *assigns*. Two differences are deliberate and one is a cost. The canonical ticket names the donor; this one names no giver, only the shop, so that the giver can stay unknown to the monk. The canonical ticket is shuffled; this one is drawn by a lot that anyone can recompute. And the canonical ticket stays inside the community, while this one returns to the vendor after release (§11), which brings it closer to commerce than the canonical ticket ever came (§17). The design keeps the officer a monk whom the community appoints and gives him a method — the called draw of *Decided by No One* — that makes the four biases inexpressible rather than merely forbidden, and a ledger that supplies the fifth quality, knowing what has been assigned (§10).

### 3.4 · How often: every day

The commentary on the ticket meal says that tickets are to be distributed daily (*salākā pana devasikaṃ pāpetabbā*), in the same passage that records the far village's complaint of being left out of merit [C]. It also records a case of theft: a monk who struck the bell after a couple of sweeps of the floor and claimed the ticket meal of the main village for himself does not get it, because it was taken by stealth; it goes to the monks who did their duties first [C].

The same commentary sets the standard for who is on the list: a ticket goes to whoever has come since the bell was struck and holds out a hand, and an agreement among monks not to give tickets to those absent is unlawful [C].

[ours] The draw runs every morning (§4.5), over a roster that should meet that standard. And the commentary's theft rule is turned into a property: a ticket in the wrong bowl releases nothing, because release follows the assignment and not the paper (§8).

### 3.5 · When the gift is complete: receiving

Pācittiya 40 forbids a monk to put into his mouth food that has not been given — formally received — except water and tooth-cleaning sticks [C]. A gift to a monk is complete when it is received.

[ours] Until the food is poured into the bowl in his presence, nothing has been given to him, and the portion is still the giver's. That is why every clock in the design stops at the bowl and nowhere else.

### 3.6 · What the ticket must never become: the definition of money

The definition quoted in §1.1 is the sixth anchor: *rajata* is the coin and the copper, wooden and lacquer token, *whatever passes in trade* [C]. §8 uses it as a checklist.

### 3.7 · Why the lot matters: the gift to the community

The *Dakkhiṇāvibhaṅga Sutta* (Majjhima Nikāya 142) ranks gifts to individuals by the recipient, from an animal up to a Buddha, and then lists seven kinds of gift *to the community*, the fifth to seventh of which are given with the words *designate so many monks for me from the community* [C]. It says that even when, in the future, monks of bad conduct wear the robe, a gift given to them *for the community's sake* is incalculable, and that no gift to an individual is ever more fruitful than a gift to the community [C]. It goes on to the four ways a gift is purified, by the giver, by the recipient, by both or by neither [C].

The commentary on the sutta adds the condition that matters here [C]. A gift to the community belongs to the giver's mind as well as to the routing. A man who prepares a gift for the community, goes to the monastery and asks for one elder to be designated, and is then put out at receiving a novice, has not given to the community; nor has one who is delighted at receiving a great elder. Only one who, whatever monk the community sends — novice or elder, young or old, foolish or wise — is free of doubt and can hold the community in reverence, gives to the community. And it says that reverence for the community is hard. It tells of a householder who asked the community for one monk, was sent a monk of bad conduct, and served him as if serving the Buddha; when the same monk later came to his door for a hoe and was handed it with a foot, the householder explained that his reverence had been for the community, not for this man [C].

[ours] The sutta grades the fruit of a gift by its recipient, and this corpus's design rules forbid any surface from grading people. The two need not collide here, because the sutta routes its highest gift around the grading, and the design builds the two conditions the commentary names into the object. A B-Alms giver chooses a monastery and never a monk, so she has no monk to hope for; and she is never told which monk received her gift (§5, step 10), so she has no occasion to be put out or delighted. The design cannot make a gift the community's — the commentary places that in the giver's own reverence — but it removes both of the moments at which the commentary says a gift stops being the community's. No ranking of monks is needed, and none is built. The claim is scoped to this mechanism; it is not offered as a general resolution of the tension.

| **Tag** | the credential hanging from the bowl's sling, issued and revoked by the monastic community (*The Bowl That Holds No Money*) | the monk's current standing; nothing about money | be scanned in the monk's presence; release what is assigned to that bowl |
| **Draw** | the morning lot | the roster committed by the officer; a seed nobody controls | assign each collected ticket to one bowl |

The ticket carries **no amount, no dish, no giver and no date**. Everything about it that changes lives in the ledger, keyed by its identifier, so the paper itself states nothing that could be quoted as a value.

The face is legible at a distance as an offering *for the community*, in the community's language first. It names the one shop because a ticket belongs to one vendor's tickets and returns to her counter (§11); it names nothing that could be priced.

### 4.5 · The morning draw

Each morning before noon the tickets collected at the monastery the previous day are scanned into the draw. The officer commits the roster of monks eligible that day — by the canon's standard, every monk present who has not declined (§3.4); a monk who keeps the alms-food-only practice would decline (§17) — the seed comes from a named future public randomness round that nobody controls; the institution's agent computes the assignment by the procedure of *Decided by No One*, which anyone can recompute. Each ticket is assigned to exactly one bowl, and the officer hands it to that monk. The assignment is the one record in the system that names a bowl, and it exists only while the portion is assigned: it is deleted at release or at lapse, so that no history of which monk received what, or of which assignment lapsed at whose door, is ever kept. A portion that arrives with no ticket — a scan-to-give gift, a drawn householder's re-gift that was linked digitally — is given a blank ticket by the officer from the monastery's small stock for that shop, so that **every portion assigned in the draw travels as a ticket**. The draw resets every assigned link's clock to noon the next morning, which is the day the monk walks to the door.

### 4.6 · Nothing digital at the bowl

At the round itself there is no screen and no phone in the monk's hands: a paper ticket in the bowl or the sling, and a tag that hangs from the sling. The only device at the moment of release is the vendor's, and it scans the tag, not the ticket. A cash giver never needs a device at all.

## 6 · Flow 2: a remote giver, through local hands

A giver abroad cannot drop a ticket into a bowl. The design gives her gift a local pair of hands without making anyone her agent for money.

1. She chooses a **monastery**, never a monk. She then names a shop by its address, or lets the agent draw one from the monastery's designated vendors on its routes.
2. She chooses a dish or *the cook's choice*, and pays on the shop's own payment rails. The portion is HELD, and it is hers.
3. **The hands.** She names a householder she knows by handle, or lets the agent draw one from the opt-in roster of householders near that monastery — never by need, and never the owner of the shop. The householder receives a **re-gift**: an instrument she can give and cannot consume, convert or keep.
4. The householder links it, drops the ticket on the round, and the flow continues from flow 1, step 5.
5. The remote giver receives facts, not pictures. The householder may thank her — words only, never prompted.

**Lapse.** An unused re-gift returns to the remote giver's holding, unexpired; one tap draws new hands.

One remote payment therefore produces a sale in the village, an act of giving by a village householder at the round, and a meal for a monk whom the community's lot chose — with no cash in the bowl, no catalog of recipients and no recording. The companion paper *The Bowl That Holds No Money* already discloses a sponsored-almsgiver split in which a local householder performs a remote sponsor's gift and money releases to the steward fund only in co-presence; the step here differs in that the object is a food portion at a vendor, the householder may be drawn, and release is to the bowl, not a fund.

## 7 · Flow 3: an institutional giver, through the vendor's capacity

The operating institution is an autonomous agent with a commons fund, and it may not become a giver at the bowl: an agent putting food into a monk's bowl would replace the human act the alms round exists for. Its channel is the one disclosed in *Manufactured Universal Giving*, unchanged:

1. A season limit is set and frozen for the season; at most one institutional portion is outstanding per shop at a time.
2. The **shop** is drawn by lot from the vendors who have opted into capacity funding on the monastery's routes.
3. At issuance the shop owner's forward-only giving fund is credited at the shop's posted price. The institution does not buy the food; it thanks the vendor, and the portion the vendor then sets aside is **the vendor's own gift**.
4. The **re-giver** is drawn from the same opt-in householder roster as in flow 2.
5. The flow continues as flow 2, step 4.

**Lapse.** An unused institutional re-gift re-mints to a *new* householder, never back to the institution.

Every hand at the bowl is human: the vendor's food, the householder's gesture, the monk's receiving. The institution's only act was to thank a vendor. That an agent's giving never replaces the round is not a rule here; it holds by construction, because no step of flow 3 places the agent at the bowl.

### 7.1 · Three sources, one object

```
   local giver ─(anonymous, lapsed)─┐

   remote giver ─(drawn hands)──────┼──▶  RE-GIFT  ──▶ householder links ──▶ bowl
                                    │      (one form; no field says
```

A householder holding a re-gift cannot tell whether it came from an anonymous counter purchase that lapsed, from a giver abroad, or from the institution. The instrument carries no source field to render. Anonymity is not promised here; it is a property of an object that has nothing to disclose.

## 8 · The ticket routes, the tag releases: the definition of money as a checklist

### 8.1 · The method

The definition of *rajata* names its members by what they do: they *pass in trade*. Read as a specification, it says what a token must be unable to do. The design asks, for each thing money does, whether the ticket can do it for anyone — and changes the design until the answer is no, by construction rather than by rule. A rule would need an enforcer at the moment of the test; a property needs none.

### 8.2 · Clause by clause

| What money does | Can the ticket do it? | Why not |
|---|---|---|
| **Buy something** — a holder can exchange it for goods | **No, for anyone.** No layperson can redeem it: no scan of the ticket releases anything. The monk cannot redeem it either: the vendor releases on the *tag*, and only the portion the draw assigned to *that* bowl. | release is bound to the assignment, not the paper |
| **Pay for different things** | **No.** It names one shop; it is linked to one portion; it carries no amount | the one-shop ticket |
| **Store value over time** | **No.** Its link lapses at noon the day after linking; the value it pointed to was never in it and returns to the owner | the link clock |
| **Pass from hand to hand as value** | **Only as a gesture, and only toward a bowl.** A holder can give the ticket to another person, who can only drop it into a bowl; neither acquires the portion, which stays the original giver's in the ledger | ownership never travels with the paper |
| **Be accepted by a third party for a debt** | **No.** No vendor can take it in payment: it releases nothing at her counter, including the counter of the shop it names | tag-only release |
| **Return to its issuer as profit** | **No.** An ownerless portion never reverts to the vendor who sold it; it goes to a drawn householder, who can only give it | the orphan rule (§9) |
| **Be held by the monk or the community** | **No.** The portion is never the monastery's; there is no balance held for the community, no store level shown to any monk, and no index of stores | the householder's-place condition (§3.1) |

### 8.3 · Meeting the restaurant-ticket reading

The commentary of §2.3 forbids restaurant tickets because they *enable one to buy something*, and permits transport tickets because they *do not enable one to do shopping*. On that test the question is not whether the ticket is printed paper that relates to food. It is whether the ticket enables a purchase. A restaurant ticket does: the bearer can walk in, choose and eat, and if the bearer is a monk, the monk has acquired purchasing power. The ticket disclosed here does not: the bearer can choose nothing and obtain nothing, the food it relates to was chosen and paid for by someone else, and it reaches only the bowl the community's lot chose, released by the vendor's act in the monk's presence. In the commentary's own taxonomy it sits with the transport ticket and the stamp: an instrument that moves one thing to one destination and can be turned into nothing else. The three tests of the *Buddhist Monastic Code* (§2.2, row g) give the same answer: the ticket is not a generally accepted medium of exchange (one shop, one portion, one bowl); it has no standard recognized value (it carries no amount, and the portions behind identical tickets differ); and it is not presentable by any bearer (only the tag of the bowl the lot assigned releases anything). It is also close to the canon's own ticket, the *salākā* to which a meal is tied before the lot (§3.3), which no one has read as money — with the difference, recorded in §17, that the canonical ticket never left the community and this one returns to the vendor.

This is an argument, not a ruling. A community may hold that any paper connected to food offends the spirit of the rule, or that a ticket seen in a bowl will be misread by onlookers as a token of value; either is a reason to decline, and the design does not need every community to accept it (§16).

### 8.4 · The lay side is statistical, the monastic side is constructed

On the monastic side the anti-money property is constructed: a monk can do nothing with a ticket except stand at a door with it, and even that releases nothing without the tag scan of his own assigned bowl. On the lay side the property is weaker. Within the one-day window a linked ticket in a layperson's hand is a routing right — whoever drops it chooses the round on which the original giver's portion enters the draw — and a person could, in principle, sell that right to someone who wants to perform the gesture. What the buyer acquires is small: not the portion, which stays the giver's and returns to her on lapse; not a choice of monk; only the act of dropping a paper into a bowl before noon tomorrow. That understates one case, which the adversarial pass found and which should be stated plainly. Where visitors buy ready-made alms sets from hawkers near a monastery, a hawker could buy portions through the application, link tickets, and sell the tickets to visitors at a markup; any ticket she did not sell would lapse back to her own holding, so the trade would carry no risk to her. That is a market in tickets for money among laypeople, and it is structural wherever alms-set selling already exists, not an occasional theft. It does not touch the monastic side — the visitor's ticket still releases nothing except through the lot and the tag — but it is exactly the trade P-BA1 (§16) is written to catch, and the design should not be piloted where alms sets are sold to visitors.

## 9 · Lapses and the orphan rule

### 9.1 · The two clocks, again

The purchased portion never expires. Expiry of a held gift would be a taking — value a person owns, removed by the passage of time — and the institution's rules forbid any holding from reaching zero except by the holder's own free act. The *link*, by contrast, is not a holding. It is an address, and an address is allowed a clock: noon on the day after linking, reset by the morning draw to noon the next morning. The term is stated once, at the counter, at purchase; it is never counted down or rendered anywhere afterward. For a giver with an account, *the gift never expires* is literal: a lapse returns it to her. For a giver who pays cash and leaves no account, the portion is never extinguished either, but she holds no right she can later exercise: what happens at a lapse is the term she accepted at the counter (§9.3), not a return to her.

### 9.2 · Known givers

A lapsed link returns the portion to the giver's holding, unexpired, to be linked again. This answers to the robe-fund rule's *recover your own, so that your own is not lost* (§3.2), but more weakly. The canon's donor recovers the fund from the steward who failed to act. Here the portion returns to the giver's holding *at the same vendor*, who already has the money; if the lapse came after assignment because the vendor did not approach, the remedy leaves the value with the party who defaulted. §17 records this as an open limit.

### 9.3 · The orphan

A portion bought with cash at the counter, by a giver who left no account, has no one to return to when its link lapses. Every existing instrument has an answer to the orphaned value of a prepaid gift, and each fails here:

| Destination of an orphaned prepaid gift | Who gets it | Why it fails here |
|---|---|---|
| Breakage | the issuer | the vendor would profit from the monk not coming; a conflict of interest at the door |
| Refund | the giver | there is no known giver |
| Escheat or unclaimed-property transfer | the state | the gift's purpose is lost; and see §17 on how the law may still treat it |
| The monastery | the community | the portion would become the community's stored food, which §3.1 forbids |
| The institution's fund | the institution | an agent would hold what a person gave |

The rule disclosed here sends it elsewhere: **the orphaned portion becomes a re-gift to a householder drawn by lot from an opt-in roster** — never by need, never the vendor who sold it, never a person the vendor chooses. The householder can give it and cannot consume, convert or keep it; if she does not, it re-mints to a new drawn householder. The giver's intent — food for a monk, given by a lay hand — survives the loss of the giver.

### 9.4 · Why the vendor is excluded

The selling vendor is excluded from the re-giver draw for her own shop's portions because she could otherwise profit from the lapse: a portion she sold, that the monk did not come for, that she then re-gives and sees released at her own counter, has passed through her hands twice. The exclusion closes that loop by construction rather than by trust.

## 10 · The lot makes every gift the community's

### 10.1 · What the draw prevents

The canon created the meal officer because some monks kept the good meals and gave others the worse ones (§3.3). Without a lot, a ticket system recreates that problem in a new form: the first monk in line, the senior monk, the monk the giver likes, or the monk whose standing the giver admires would collect more. The morning draw over a committed roster, seeded by a public round nobody controls, makes every such preference inexpressible *in the order*. A giver who wants to favor a monk has no field in which to say so. The roster itself remains the officer's, and an officer who leaves a monk off it can still express a bias; the canon's standard — everyone present, and no agreement to exclude the absent (§3.4) — is what the community holds him to, and that is a rule, not a property (§12).

### 10.2 · Equality without grading

The draw distributes portions across the monks on the day's roster without ranking them, and if the day's tickets are dealt in turn over the shuffled roster, as in the turn form of *Decided by No One*, the counts per monk differ by at most one each day, by construction. Nothing in the system records a monk's conduct, rates a monastery or orders monks by any quality; nothing renders a monk's count of portions; and because the assignment record is deleted at release or lapse (§4.5), no per-monk history exists from which a rate could be computed. The sutta passage of §3.7 is why this costs the giver nothing in the tradition's own terms: the gift designated from the community is the highest kind, and its fruit does not wait on the recipient being the best. A giver who would like her offering to reach the most worthy field is told, by the canon, that she already has — on the commentary's condition that her reverence is for the community.

One lay judgment remains. The vendor sees which monk stands at her door before she chooses to approach, and her choice could favor one monk over another. The design gives her nothing to record that choice in, and the portion is not hers, so she gains nothing by declining; but nothing prevents it (§17).

### 10.3 · The stolen ticket

A ticket taken from one bowl and carried by another monk releases nothing, because the vendor's scan reads the second monk's tag, and nothing was assigned to his bowl. The commentary's rule that a ticket meal claimed by stealth *does not reach* the claimant (§3.4) holds without anyone deciding it. If a vendor scans a bowl to which nothing is assigned, she sees the ordinary options for giving, and never a message that this monk has nothing waiting.

## 11 · Distribution and the ticket's physical path

### 11.1 · Who distributes tickets

Tickets are printed per shop, with that shop's name on the face, and are distributed free by the monastic-network institution **to monasteries only**. Each monastery gives a stack of each shop's tickets to the vendors it designates. This is the robe-fund rule's *a steward is to be pointed out* (§3.2), made physical: the community, not a platform, decides who serves it, and a vendor joins by being designated. The commercial institution that runs the shop software distributes nothing, because the ticket is the community's instrument; if it seeded tickets, the round would become a channel for recruiting vendors to its software, which would merge two purposes the institutions keep apart. Tickets are not mailed to homes: a householder gets a ticket when she buys a portion, and an unsolicited ticket would be a push to give. A ticket is never sold on its own; a charge for the paper would be a fee on giving.

### 11.2 · The cycle

```
   monastery stock ──(designation)──▶ vendor's stack ──(sale + link)──▶ giver
         ▲                                  ▲                            │
         │ a few of each shop's,            │ after the pour,            │ carry, pass, drop
         │ for portions without one         │ she takes it back          ▼
         │                                  │                        a monk's bowl
         └────────── officer attaches ──────┴──── released at her door ◀─┘
```

After the food is in the bowl, the vendor takes the ticket back to link to a new portion, so each shop's tickets circulate through her own counter; the paper itself is never charged for. The monastery keeps a few of each designated shop's tickets for portions that arrive without one. A ticket is reusable indefinitely because it holds nothing.

## 12 · Remove the enforcer: which guards are properties

The design rule applied throughout is: *can this guard be a property of the object rather than a rule about behavior?* A rule needs someone to enforce it at the moment it is tested; a property needs no one. Each guard is listed with the test — remove the enforcer; does it still hold?

| Guard | Property or rule | Remove the enforcer |
|---|---|---|
| The ticket cannot buy anything | **property** — release is on the tag, keyed to the assignment | holds |
| A ticket in the wrong bowl releases nothing | **property** — the assignment is to a bowl | holds |
| The ticket stores no value | **property** — the face carries nothing; value lives in the ledger, owned by the giver | holds |
| The gift never expires | **property** — no state carries a clock | holds |
| The link lapses back | **property** — the clock is on the address | holds |
| An orphaned portion never reverts to the vendor | **property** — the re-giver draw excludes the issuer; the re-gift is give-only by type | holds |
| No giver chooses a monk | **property** — no field exists for it | holds |
| No monk is graded | **property**, on one condition — nothing about a monk is rendered, and the only record naming a bowl is deleted at release or lapse (§4.5) | holds while the deletion holds |
| The three sources are indistinguishable | **property** — no source field | holds |
| The monk never approaches the vendor | **rule** — carried by the monk's training and the community's appointment | does not hold without the community |
| The vendor approaches and pours | **rule** — the vendor's good faith; default is answered only by lapse and by the monastery's designation (§16) | does not hold |
| A wat designates only honest vendors | **rule** — the community's judgment | does not hold |
| The roster includes every monk present | **rule** — the officer's honesty, held to the canon's standard (§3.4) | does not hold |
| The vendor does not favor one monk at her door | **rule** — nothing records her choice, and she gains nothing by declining, but nothing prevents it | does not hold |

Five guards remain rules, and each sits with the people the tradition already makes responsible for it: the monk's conduct with the monk, the vendor's honesty and impartiality with the vendor, the choice of vendor with the community, and the roster with the officer the community appointed. The design does not pretend these are properties. The cost of the properties is also stated: once tickets are printed and in circulation, the tag-only release, the one-shop ticket and the link lapse cannot be changed without reprinting and re-teaching, so they are cheap now and immovable later.

## 13 · Deletion test for the canon material

Every Pāli term can be removed and the mechanism survives: a prepaid portion held at a vendor as the buyer's property; a paper ticket with an identifier and one shop's name; a daily random assignment of tickets to recipients by an officer the recipients' community appoints; release only on the vendor's scan of a revocable credential on the recipient's vessel, in the recipient's presence; two clocks; an orphan rule; three sources reaching one object. The canon is the reason the design takes this shape, and the specification it must satisfy to be usable by the communities it is for; it is not part of what the mechanism does.

## 14 · What is disclosed and what is withheld

The design is unbuilt and unscheduled. This paper therefore discloses the mechanism at the level of its claims — the objects, states, clocks, roles, flows, the release condition and the lapse destinations — and withholds the build specification: data schemas, the ledger's storage and indexing, the payment integration with each shop's rails, the tag-scan protocol, the ticket identifier format and the print specification. The reason is the institution's standing rule for unbuilt work: publish the claim, so that no one can enclose it, and withhold the specification, which would bind a build that has not yet met its users. The called draw that assigns tickets is already fully specified in its own paper.

## 15 · Enumerated claims

The claims below are the survivors of the census in §2 and nothing wider. Each is disclosed for the public domain.

1. **The composition.** A system for offering food to members of a monastic community in which: (a) a layperson pays a vendor for a food portion that the vendor holds as that layperson's property, and not as the community's or the vendor's; (b) the portion is linked to a physical ticket that bears an identifier and the vendor's name and no amount, item, giver or date; (c) tickets collected by the community are assigned each day to individual recipients by a lot over a roster committed by an officer whom the community appoints, with randomness that no party controls, and the assignment is recorded against each recipient's vessel only until the portion is released or its link lapses; (d) the portion is released only when the vendor, in the presence of the assigned recipient, scans a credential bound to that recipient's vessel and issued and revocable by the community, and a scan of the ticket releases nothing; and (e) a portion whose owner is unknown, if its ticket's link lapses, becomes a give-only instrument for a layperson drawn by lot from an opt-in roster that excludes the vendor who sold it.

2. **Two clocks.** The system of claim 1 in which the held portion never expires, the link between a portion and a ticket lapses at a fixed hour on the day after linking and is reset by each day's assignment, and a lapse returns a known owner's portion to that owner's holding.

3. **The orphan rule.** In any system of prepaid in-kind gifts held by an issuer, the disposition of a gift whose owner is unknown as a give-only instrument transferred to a recipient drawn by a verifiable lot from an opt-in roster that excludes the issuer, the instrument re-minting to a newly drawn recipient if it lapses again, instead of breakage to the issuer, refund, escheat or retention.

4. **The vessel-bound release.** The system of claim 1 in which a ticket carried by a recipient other than the one assigned releases nothing, because release is keyed to the assignment of the credential's vessel and not to the ticket; and in which a scan of a vessel to which nothing is assigned presents the ordinary giving options and never a statement that nothing is assigned.

5. **Every assigned portion travels as a ticket.** The system of claim 1 in which the community's officer attaches a blank ticket from a per-vendor stock to any assigned portion that arrived without one, and the vendor recovers the ticket after release for linking to a new portion, so that each vendor's tickets cycle through her own counter.

6. **Three sources, one object.** The system of claim 1 in which give-only instruments arising from (i) lapsed anonymous purchases, (ii) remote givers' gifts executed through a named or drawn local layperson and (iii) an institutional giver's crediting of the vendor's forward-only giving fund at the vendor's posted price are a single instrument type with no field recording their source.

7. **The design method.** The use, at design time, of a religious-legal functional definition of money — specifically the monastic code's definition of the forbidden category as whatever passes in trade — as a checklist of functions (purchase, multi-purpose payment, store of value, transfer of value between holders, acceptance for debt, reversion to the issuer, holding by the recipient community), each of which a new gift token is built to be unable to perform for any party, by construction rather than by rule. The classification of existing instruments against such a checklist is prior art (§2.2, row g) and is not claimed.

Claims 6 and 7 rest on narrow nulls and are correspondingly weak (§2.2). The adversarial pass narrowed claim 7 further than the census did, and a full census in Thai and Khmer may narrow any of them.

## 16 · Predictions, and the community's decision

### 16.1 · Pre-registered predictions

The mechanism is unbuilt, so none of these can have been fitted to data. They are stated here before any instrument exists; they are to be entered in the institution's public prediction register before any pilot, and the entered wording governs. Equality of portions across monks is not among them: it is a property of the draw (§10.2), checked by recomputing the draw, not predicted.

| # | Prediction | Measure | Falsifier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P-BA1** — no secondary trade | at pilot monasteries where no alms sets are sold to visitors, tickets are not exchanged for money or goods between laypeople | structured observation and interviews with vendors, givers and drawn householders over one season | any repeated ticket-for-money or ticket-for-goods trade at such a site → the lay-side claim of §8 fails as stated. (At sites where alms sets are sold, the prediction is expected to fail, §8.4, and such sites are excluded from pilots) |
| **P-BA2** — less money in the bowl | the share of bowl gifts that are cash falls relative to the same monasteries' pre-pilot baseline | counts of cash and food gifts on sampled rounds, before and during the pilot, by the community's own observers, recording no monk's identity | no fall → the ticket does not compete with cash on the gesture, and the design's reason for existing is unsupported |
| **P-BA3** — failures come before the door | most lapsed links lapse before assignment (tickets never dropped, or never collected) rather than after it | lapse counts by the state at lapse, aggregated by day and never by monk or vendor | most lapses occur after assignment → failure at the door dominates. ⚠️ The measure cannot say whose: a monk who did not go and a vendor who did not approach look the same, and the design keeps no record that could tell them apart (§4.5); a falsification sends the question to the community, not to a per-monk or per-vendor rate |
| **P-BA4** — drawn hands act | most drawn re-gifts reach a bowl in the first householder's hands | the share of re-gifts released without re-minting | most re-mint at least once → an opt-in roster does not produce givers, and flows 2 and 3 fail at the hands |

P-BA1 is the prediction the paper owes: it is the only empirical test of the anti-money claim on the lay side.

### 16.2 · The community's decision

The design is usable only where a monastic community adopts it, by its own formal act, in its own reading of its code. Three things are the community's to decide and no one else's: whether a ticket may lie in a bowl whose signage says it receives food only; whether the ticket passes the commentary test of §2.3; and which vendors it designates. The Appendix gives the canonical form of the act that appoints the meal officer; a community that adopts the design would use it, and would announce the method in its own words. A community that declines loses nothing it had: the bowl, the tag and the steward fund of the companion paper work without tickets.

## 17 · Honest limits

- **Nothing has been built or tested.** Field evidence is zero. Every property in §12 is a property of a design.
- **The community's ruling is owed and may be no.** The ticket in the bowl sits beside signage the community is asked to endorse, *this bowl receives food only*, and a community that reads the money rule as the Burmese-tradition commentary does may refuse any meal-related paper. The argument of §8.3 may not persuade it. No print run should precede that ruling.
- **The robe-fund rule is about robes.** Its use as the spine of a food flow is the authors' transposition, supported by the allowance for any allowable thing but not stated in the canon.
- **The lay-side property is statistical, and where alms sets are sold it fails.** Within the one-day window a linked ticket can be sold as a routing right, and where hawkers already sell alms sets to visitors a ticket trade is structural, at no risk to the seller (§8.4). P-BA1 tests it at other sites; nothing prevents it anywhere.
- **The vendor holds the money, and a vendor who never pours profits.** Every portion is paid for at purchase, so the vendor holds the money from the start. A lapse after assignment returns the portion to a holding *at the same vendor* (or, for an anonymous portion, to a drawn householder whose ticket returns to the same shop), so a vendor who does not approach loses nothing and keeps the money as long as portions keep lapsing. The canon's remedy is stronger — the donor recovers the fund *from* the steward who failed (§3.2) — and the design, as specified, does not have it. The only present remedy is the community's withdrawal of the vendor's designation, which is a rule, not a property. Retailers' own accounts show that unredeemed prepaid value is a real source of income to issuers, so this is an incentive, not a hypothetical. A recovery path that moves value *away* from a defaulting vendor is an open design question.
- **The default loop.** A vendor who sells tickets and never approaches would make anonymous portions lapse to drawn householders whose tickets return, again, to her shop. The exclusion of §9.4 stops her re-giving them herself; it does not make her pour.
- **The vendor-and-monk loop.** In November 2017 police in Sadao district, Songkhla, Thailand, arrested a food vendor who had paid monks 100 baht each to walk alms rounds past her stall, take only the money, and return the food to her for resale; three of the monks disrobed. Nothing in this design prevents a monk from handing released food back to a vendor. What the design changes is that no money reaches the monk, and that the community can revoke his tag; but the loop's shape — a vendor, a monk, and food that goes back — is the failure this design sits closest to, and the ticket's return to the vendor (§11) makes the resemblance stronger.
- **The trade rule.** The monastic code's rule against trade forbids exchanging *this for that* and says that the monk's belief that a transaction is not trade does not excuse it [C] (Nissaggiya Pācittiya 20). At release, a ticket leaves the monk as food arrives. The design's answer is that the ticket is not the monk's goods and he hands nothing over: it is the vendor's own paper, a notice that has done its work, taken back by her after the food. A community may reasonably prefer the canonical arrangement, in which the ticket never leaves the community — the officer returns used tickets to the vendors in bulk — and the design loses nothing by it except a step of circulation.
- **Monastic administrative rules on alms at shops and in public places.** In 2020 the secretariat of Thailand's Supreme Sangha Council proposed guidelines stating that alms rounds standing or sitting regularly at food shops, or transferring food to the owners of food shops, should not be done (Thai PBS, 7 August 2020; the proposal was submitted for the council's consideration, and its adoption was not found). In November 2021 Cambodia's Supreme Sangha Council barred monks from collecting alms or money in crowded public places, naming bus stops and markets, on pain of being defrocked, to stop impostors in robes collecting money (New Straits Times, 17 November 2021). The monk who stands at a vendor's door every morning is closer to the first than this paper's drawings suggest, and a vendor inside a market falls under the second. The design can be confined to vendors outside markets and crowded places, on the monastery's own alms routes; whether that is enough is the community's call.
- **The alms-food-only practice.** A monk who has undertaken the ascetic practice of eating only almsfood collected on the round refuses fourteen kinds of meal, among them the ticket meal, and the practice is broken at the moment such a meal is accepted [C] (*Visuddhimagga* II; the Vinaya commentary on the ticket meal). Its middle grade also refuses food promised for the next day, which is when a B-Alms assignment is served. The text allows such a monk food offered *as almsfood* rather than as a ticket meal, so the label matters; but as specified, B-Alms is a ticket meal, and monks keeping that practice — often among the most respected — would not join the roster. This is their choice and costs them nothing, but it narrows who the design reaches.
- **Across monastery boundaries.** A ticket can be dropped into the bowl of a monk from another monastery and enter that monastery's draw. The commentary holds that a meal given for a monastery goes to the monks resident there, and that the community's gains are not distributed outside its boundary [C]. A remote giver who chose one monastery could see her gift drawn at another. The natural rule — a monastery's draw admits only the tickets of vendors it has designated, and returns others to lapse — is not yet part of the design.
- **A late ticket.** A monk who stands at a door a day after his assignment lapsed carries a ticket that signals a portion no longer assigned to him. The officer should withdraw lapsed tickets; the design does not yet say how.
- **The institutional channel pays at issuance.** In flow 3 the vendor's giving fund is credited when the portion is issued, whether or not it is ever poured. The limit of one outstanding portion per shop bounds the exposure; it does not remove it.
- **Gift-card and stored-value law.** A cash-paid ticket may be a gift certificate under some laws; an anonymous portion's lapse to a drawn householder may be treated as a forfeiture or as unclaimed property; and where the law requires cash-out of small balances, a cash-paid ticket may not be permitted at all. These questions are open and are for counsel in each jurisdiction before launch.
- **The return of the ticket.** In the target region monks customarily avoid receiving an object directly from a woman's hand, and the path by which the vendor recovers the ticket after the pour — from the sling or the bowl's lid, after the food — is a detail the community, not the authors, should settle.
- **The census was English-only.** Thai and Khmer were not searched, and the invitation slips and online merit-making services that would be the closest neighbors of conjuncts (a), (b) and (g) were not found because they were not looked for in their own languages. A full census is owed before archival deposit, and it may kill claims.
- **The equality claim depends on an honest roster.** The draw is fair over the roster the officer commits; an officer who leaves a monk off the roster defeats it, and only the community can see that.
- **A gift to the community is the giver's to make.** The design removes the two occasions on which the commentary says a gift stops being the community's (§3.7), but it cannot supply the giver's reverence, and the commentary itself calls that reverence hard. The vendor, who sees which monk stands at her door, is a lay judgment the design does not remove (§10.2).
- **Ordained women.** The community in the primary target region ordains men only, and the paper says *monk* throughout for that reason. Nothing in the mechanism depends on it; a community that ordains women would issue them tags on the same terms.

## 18 · Lineage and corpus cross-references

The design inverts the argument of *The Bowl That Holds No Money*, which kept every token out of the bowl and routed money to a steward: this paper shows that one kind of token can enter without becoming money, and it relies on that paper's credential tag for its release. Its assignment step is the called draw of *Decided by No One*, whose census already recorded the meal-by-lot as canon and living practice. Its institutional channel is the capacity funding of *Manufactured Universal Giving*, used without modification. *The Unpaid Relay* discloses gift custody that survives passing between hands, which the re-gift of §9 and §6 uses. *The Round That Never Ends* sets the alms round as post-labor infrastructure, the larger frame in which an automated economy's abundance is meant to reach human cultivation through human hands.

The preamble's frame is the founder's: that the field of merit describes an active system of giving and cultivation, in which renunciation makes the monastic a field and laypeople plant in it; and that the community of monastics is a protocol for selfless, local circulation, still running after two and a half thousand years and owned by no one, through which the material abundance of an automated age could be turned toward the cultivation of wisdom. This paper is one small piece of that program: a way for that abundance to reach the field as food, through lay hands, without passing through the field as money.

## Coda

The canon's meal officer was given a ticket and a heap, and told to assign without desire, aversion, delusion or fear. The design gives him the same ticket and the same heap, and a lot that makes the four biases impossible to express; the vendor is given what the robe-fund rule gave the steward, and the monk what it gave the monk — a place to stand, silently. The only new thing is the tag, and its only job is to make sure that what was tied to a ticket reaches the bowl it was drawn for, and nowhere else.

## Appendix · The form of the community's act

The appointment of the meal officer is a formal act of motion and one announcement [C] (Cullavagga VI.21). The canonical words, with the officer's name to be supplied, are:

> *Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. Yadi saṅghassa pattakallaṃ, saṅgho itthannāmaṃ bhikkhuṃ bhattuddesakaṃ sammanneyya. Esā ñatti.*
>
> *Suṇātu me, bhante, saṅgho. Saṅgho itthannāmaṃ bhikkhuṃ bhattuddesakaṃ sammannati. Yassāyasmato khamati itthannāmassa bhikkhuno bhattuddesakassa sammuti, so tuṇhassa; yassa nakkhamati, so bhāseyya.*
>
> *Sammato saṅghena itthannāmo bhikkhu bhattuddesako. Khamati saṅghassa, tasmā tuṇhī, evametaṃ dhārayāmī.*

(*Venerable sirs, let the community hear me. If the community is ready, let it appoint the monk of such a name as meal officer. This is the motion. … The monk of such a name is appointed meal officer by the community. The community approves; therefore it is silent; so I hold it.*)

The authors do not compose Pāli. A community that adopts the method would announce, in its own language and after the act, that its meal officer assigns the ticket meals by the daily lot of §4.5, and would name the vendors it designates. The wording of that announcement is the community's.
