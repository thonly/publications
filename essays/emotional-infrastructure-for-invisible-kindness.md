## What I see when I think about anonymous nearby tipping

I want to write about the full vision I see for anonymous nearby tipping — the clarity of which is the reason I have not given up on HeartBank for more than twenty years.

At first glance, it can sound like a payment mechanism, a generosity app, or a social experiment. But that is not what I see when I think about it deeply. I see a new emotional layer for human society: an *infrastructure for invisible kindness* operating continuously in the background of everyday life.

Most systems in modern life are designed around explicit exchange. Credit is tracked. Reputation is attached. Recognition is optimized. Every action points back toward identity, status, and social proof. Even generosity is often transformed into performance. But anonymous nearby tipping introduces something radically different: the ability for human warmth to move through physical spaces without ego claiming ownership over it.

## A restaurant, an anonymous tip, and what actually happens

I imagine a restaurant setting where an anonymous guest leaves an unexpectedly generous tip for a waitress. What makes the moment powerful is not just the money, but the uncertainty around where it came from. The credit doesn't attach itself to a single visible person. Instead, it diffuses outward across the entire social space of the restaurant.

From the waitress's perspective, the kindness becomes ambient. It could have been the quiet couple in the corner, the regular at the bar, the family she joked with earlier, or someone she barely noticed passing by. Every nearby interaction becomes newly charged with possibility. The anonymity transforms the gesture from a transaction between two identifiable people into a broader experience of human goodwill.

That changes the emotional texture of the moment. A named tip can feel personal and appreciated, but anonymous generosity carries a different psychological weight. Because there is no obvious social reward, no performance, and no claim to recognition, the act feels purer — more like a spontaneous expression of care from one human being to another. The waitress is left not with gratitude toward a single individual, but with a heightened sense that the world around her may contain unseen kindness.

There is also something uniquely powerful about proximity. The generosity did not arrive from an abstract institution or a distant donor; it came from someone physically present in her immediate environment, someone who occupied the same atmosphere and shared the same brief slice of life. The combination of nearness and anonymity creates a strange emotional effect: the restaurant itself begins to feel warmer, more humane, almost protective.

## Extending the principle into the wider world

Now imagine extending that principle into the wider world through something like a *Thank All Nearby* button. One tap, one intentional act, instantly shared with every person within a certain space: every homeless person gathered in an alley or shelter line, every family eating in a church hall, every student sitting in a classroom, every exhausted worker at the end of a long shift.

The beauty of it is that the gratitude would not arrive as a targeted reward system sorting people into winners and losers. It would arrive as an atmosphere. A field of acknowledgment. Everyone nearby would feel touched by the possibility that another human being, physically close to them, chose to express appreciation without demanding recognition, status, or reciprocity.

That changes the emotional geometry of public life.

Right now, many shared spaces feel emotionally thin. People move through each other defensively, isolated inside private concerns, uncertain whether they are seen, valued, or safe. But anonymous, proximity-based generosity creates a different social texture. It introduces warmth into places that normally feel transactional or invisible. A street corner becomes a site of human recognition. A classroom becomes more than an institutional container. A shelter becomes more than an emergency resource. The environment itself starts to feel inhabited by goodwill.

And because the kindness is diffuse, people begin relating differently to everyone around them. If appreciation could have come from anyone nearby, then *every stranger becomes newly humanized*. Suspicion softens. Curiosity increases. Small interactions matter more. Eye contact matters more. Presence matters more.

The downstream effects could be enormous. Humans might venture outside more often, linger in public spaces longer, speak to strangers more easily, and perform more spontaneous acts of care. Not because they are maximizing social capital or building a personal brand, but because they begin to experience society as emotionally alive again. Kindness becomes contagious precisely because it is not possessive.

## What this is, underneath

There is something ancient in this. Human beings evolved not only needing food and shelter, but needing evidence that they mattered to one another. The deepest forms of suffering are often forms of social invisibility: feeling unseen, unwanted, replaceable, or emotionally abandoned. A system that allows people to anonymously radiate appreciation into their immediate surroundings counteracts that invisibility in a direct and almost primal way.

This is not a new insight. The contemplative and ethical traditions have been pointing at this for millennia. Frankl, surviving the camps, named the felt sense of mattering as central to human meaning — even under the most extreme conditions, what kept people alive was the persistence of relational meaning, the sense of being held in someone's awareness. Buber called this the *I-Thou* relation, the immediate human-to-human encounter that is structurally different from any encounter with the world as object. Levinas located moral responsibility itself in the face of the other — the irreducible presence of another human is the ethical primitive from which everything else follows. Aristotle named *philia* as one of the central forms of human flourishing; Aquinas and the Christian tradition developed *agape* as the highest form of love; the Buddhist *brahmavihāra* tradition holds *mettā* — loving-kindness as a felt-sense practice — as one of the four immeasurable states central to awakening.

What the architecture I am describing does is operationalize, at the technical layer, a thesis that these traditions have already articulated in different vocabularies. The Thank-All-Nearby primitive is the engineering layer of a philosophy the contemplative traditions have been developing for two and a half millennia.

And the empirical literature now points at the same insight from a different direction. Attachment theory (Bowlby, Ainsworth, and the modern adult-attachment work) has established that the felt sense of being held in caregiver awareness is foundational to human development and persists in adult relationship structures as a determinant of psychological resilience. Social Baseline Theory (Coan and colleagues) has demonstrated that the proximity of attached others measurably reduces neural threat response in the brain — the felt sense of being held is not metaphorical, but neurologically encoded. Loneliness epidemiology (Cigna's surveys, the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory) has quantified the population-scale prevalence of loneliness in wealthy societies and its association with mortality, immune function, cognitive aging, and mental-health outcomes at the order of magnitude of major chronic diseases.

The contemplative traditions and the empirical literature converge on the same thing. The architecture is what happens when you take the convergence seriously and ask what it would mean to build infrastructure for it.

## The deepest claim

The most meaningful part may be that no explanation is attached. The recipient does not know exactly who cared, only that someone did. And paradoxically, that uncertainty can make the experience feel larger, almost transcendent. The love no longer belongs to one individual ego. It feels like it emerged from humanity itself.

Maybe that feeling — the unmistakable sense of being loved, acknowledged, or held in the awareness of another human being — is not merely one pleasure among many. Maybe it is the experience people are ultimately searching for beneath everything else: the felt sense of true love from another human being. Not abstractly, not rhetorically, but emotionally, immediately, and undeniably real. Maybe true love, in its simplest form, is the thing that makes existence feel real and worth inhabiting in the first place — and perhaps, at the deepest level of all, the meaning of life itself.

I want to be careful with this claim. It is the strongest thing I am willing to say in this essay, and I am hedging it deliberately. *Perhaps.* *At the deepest level of all.* I am not claiming that the felt sense of being held is *the* meaning of life. I am claiming that it may be *the underlying thing all the other framings are getting at* — the experience that Frankl, Buber, Levinas, Aristotle, Aquinas, and the Buddhist traditions have each named in their own vocabularies, the experience that attachment theory and social-baseline theory and loneliness epidemiology are each measuring from their own angles, the experience that anonymous nearby generosity makes available without performance, without claim, without ego.

If the convergence is real — if all these lineages are pointing at the same thing — then the architecture's deepest claim is that infrastructure for this experience is what the next phase of social life most needs, and what no existing system delivers at scale.

## Why I have not stopped

I am writing this essay now, in May of 2026, because the technical substrate for what I have just described is finally available. Verifiable human presence at the moment of action (Proof of Humanity ℠), proximity verification at the radio layer (Bluetooth Low Energy plus Apple Nearby Interaction plus Google Nearby Connections), recipient-side filtering, bilateral uncacheable anonymity, on-chain settlement of the verified-platform receipts that close the social-media authenticity-proof gap — all of these are mature enough now to compose into the Thank-All-Nearby primitive. The architecture has been quietly converging toward this for years; the technical pieces are finally in place.

I have not given up on this vision in twenty years because I have not been able to find anywhere else that the felt sense of being held is treated as load-bearing infrastructure rather than as a side effect of other systems. UBI does not deliver it; cash is impersonal. Charity does not deliver it; the asymmetry of giver-known/recipient-known erases the diffusion. Social media does not deliver it; performance contaminates everything. Therapy delivers it within a single dyad, expensively and at human-bandwidth scale, but is not infrastructure. The contemplative traditions deliver it through practice, but the practice has not become available at population scale in the modern world.

What I am building tries to address the same need at the *architectural* layer rather than at the individual-practice layer. Not as a substitute for individual practice — practice is irreplaceable; nothing scales the way meditation does for the person who meditates — but as an *ambient field* that supports the experience for the many people who will never sit down to practice, and that supports the practitioners themselves by making their shared physical spaces resonate with the same posture they cultivate internally.

The mechanism is technical. The thesis is not. The thesis is that the felt sense of being held in another human's awareness is — possibly, at the deepest level — the experience humans search for beneath everything else, and that infrastructure for that experience is the work of this generation.

This is what I have spent twenty years trying to find a way to build. The technical conditions are now favorable. I am building it.
