> *Born into the aftermath of what was lost and shaped by its silence, the "Metteyya" thing to do is fill it with song and loving-kindness again.*

## 1. A sadness with no return address

For most of my life I have carried a melancholy I could not explain. It is not depression; it does not stop me; people who know me would describe someone cheerful, driven, even relentless. But it is there, underneath, the way a river is there under a bridge you cross every day. When I began writing music, years before I understood any of this, I named my four instrumental albums after the seasons — and without noticing what I was doing, I put the same word in front of every one: *Last Spring. Last Summer. Last Autumn. Last Winter.* A whole year of lasts. A heart that holds the present as if it were already a memory. I could not have told you why.

And there was one more clue, which took me the longest to read. When I listen to the great Cambodian singers of my parents' generation — Sinn Sisamouth, whose voice every Khmer person alive knows the way they know their own name; Ros Serey Sothea, whom the king called the Queen with the Golden Voice — I cry. Not sometimes. Reliably, helplessly, from the first phrases. Here is what makes that strange: I never heard them. I was born in 1985. They were gone — killed, both of them, in the years my country does not describe so much as date — before I existed. Whatever it is in me that answers their voices did not learn its answer from any evening I sat by a radio. The answer was in me before the question arrived.

I want to tell you what I eventually understood about that sadness, because I no longer believe it is only mine — and because understanding it changed what I am doing with my life.

## 2. What was lost

Between 1975 and 1979, my country came as close as any society in modern history to losing everything a culture is made of. I will state the numbers plainly and briefly, because this essay is not about the horror; it is about what happened afterward. And I will say once, rather than hedge each figure as it comes, that they are estimates: a regime that destroys the records destroys the count along with them, and the ranges historians give are wide. Of some sixty-five thousand Buddhist monks, a remnant survived that historians count in hundreds or a few thousand. Thousands of temples were destroyed or profaned. The great majority of the nation's Buddhist manuscripts and books — including copies of the Tipiṭaka, the canon my tradition has carried for two thousand years — were burned. And the artists: the masters of the royal dance, the musicians, the filmmakers of what Cambodians call our Golden Age — the singers whose records filled every market in the sixties — were killed in proportions their traditions still count in single-digit lists of survivors.

Temples destroyed. Monks killed. Books burned. I choose those three phrases deliberately, because they are the three losses my tradition itself names when it imagines the end of the teaching. The Buddha taught that the Dharma, like everything conditioned, will not last forever — that a day comes when the teaching is lost, and that when it is wholly gone from the world, after a long darkness, the next Buddha will arise. His name is already given in the canon: Metteyya. It comes from *mettā* — loving-kindness. The next Buddha is the Buddha of loving-kindness, and he comes when the silence is total.

I grew up inside a question I did not know I was asking: what is it like to be born just after the silence?

## 3. What the canon already knew

Years into building what I am building, I learned — from the discourse called the *Cakkavatti-Sīhanāda Sutta*, which I had somehow never read — that the canon does not only imagine the loss of the teaching in the abstract. It narrates the collapse. It describes a nadir it calls the interval of the sword: a time in which human beings look at one another and see beasts, and slaughter what they see. And it describes, with a tenderness that undid me the first time I heard it summarized, who ends that time: the people who fled to the wilderness and hid. When the killing exhausts itself, they come out of the forests, see how few they are, and make a vow together — *let us do good.* From that vow, the text says, everything slowly returns: restraint first, then virtue, then welfare, generation upon generation, until the world is whole enough for the next great arrivals.

I do not need to explain to any Cambodian reader why I cannot read that passage as mythology. The people who hid in the forests and came out and vowed to do good are not figures in a cosmological story to me. They are my parents' generation. Some of them re-ordained as monks, receiving ordination again from lineages that had survived in other countries, because a lineage, once broken locally, can only be re-lit from a flame carried elsewhere. Some of them taught dance to children in refugee camps, because the choreography existed nowhere anymore except in their bodies. Some of them had carried cassette tapes out on their backs, and because of them, a boy born in 1985 could one day press play and hear a voice from before the breaking — and find, to his own bewilderment, that his body already knew it.

The teaching was not lost. It was interrupted. And everything that came back, came back the same way: **from what the survivors carried in their bodies, along channels no fire could reach.**

## 4. Recognition before memory

Here is the fact that finally gave my sadness its name, and it comes not from scripture but from a laboratory. Newborn infants recognize melodies they heard in the womb. Play a melody regularly to a child before it is born, and after birth the child's brain answers that melody differently, though the child has never heard it in the world and will never remember learning it. What a mother sings before memory, the child keeps below memory. Recognition without recall. The knowing that precedes the knower.

Now widen the aperture. A child born into a family of survivors — into a country of survivors — absorbs more than lullabies before memory. It absorbs the held breath. The silences at certain questions. The dates no one explains. The grief that is never performed and never absent, the way humidity is never absent. There is a word for this, and it has an author. Marianne Hirsch, a child of Holocaust survivors, came to *postmemory* to name what the generation after bears toward experiences they "remember" only by means of "the stories, images, and behaviors among which they grew up," transmitted "so deeply and affectively as to seem to constitute memories in their own right." Her ground is the Holocaust; a footnote to her 2008 article lists the Cambodian genocide among the other places where inherited trauma has come to explain something. Every earlier version of this essay used her word without her name. The Cambodian diaspora's children know the thing itself whether or not they ever learn the word. My melancholy was never unexplained. It was *unattributed*. I had been carrying an interrupted song my whole life: a country's worth of love and grief, sung into me before I could remember, waiting — like every song planted before memory — for something in the world to play it back.

That is what the feeling says, and I am leaving it on the page. But Hirsch's word does not say all of it. Postmemory's connection to the past, she writes, is "not actually mediated by recall but by imaginative investment, projection, and creation." Stories, images, behaviors: those arrive after birth, in a child already reaching for them. The womb is my simile, not her mechanism. And the laboratory is smaller than I made it. In the study I can point to, ten newborns whose mothers had played "Twinkle Twinkle Little Star" through the last trimester showed stronger electrical responses to its notes than infants who had not heard it (Partanen and colleagues, 2013). That is what was measured: a melody, repeated, and a brain's response. I first called this settled science. No grief crossed anything in that experiment, and the step from a nursery tune to a country's sorrow is mine. I also said in §1 that no evening by a radio taught me my answer to those voices. I cannot know that. A child keeps no log of what was playing in the next room. And a cassette of Ros Serey Sothea is the kind of object Hirsch is wary of: even for the children of survivors, she says, pictures are "no more than spaces of projection, approximation, and affiliation," and a footnote gives the tape recorder the same "promises and the frustrations." The cassette proves she sang. It cannot prove that what I hear in it is hers.

Others press harder than she does. Gary Weissman objects that "no degree of power or monumentality can transform one person's lived memories into another's." Ernst van Alphen says of the children of survivors that "the indexical relationship that defines memory has never existed." Hirsch's answer to both is a concession: "of course we do not have literal 'memories' of others' experiences." And the silence I keep leaning on is contested too. Eva Hoffman, herself a daughter of survivors, refuses the picture of parents wrapped in it: "But they also spoke," she writes, in "the sounds of nightmares, the idioms of sighs and illness." That is nearer to the held breath than a lullaby is. Writing of Cambodia, the anthropologist Sina Emde, who came to this work with her own family's silence, finds that "silence in Cambodia did not mean the absence of memory, but it also did not transmit much information about the past," and warns against a "euro-centric bias" about what such silence means. Pete Pin, a photographer and in his own words "a son of the Killing Fields," writes that such conversations "were non-existent at home," and that many second-generation Cambodians he interviewed learned the history "through secondary sources." So the silence I call one thing is not one thing. For some children it may carry a song. For others it may be a blank, filled in later from a screen. Hirsch has since warned her own field that staying with the unspeakable "risks obscuring the voices of those witnesses who, committed to passing it on for the future, do tell their story as best they can." The people in §3 who carried the tapes out on their backs were not silent. I should not let my word *silence* say they were.

The sentence of hers I cannot get past is about cost. "To grow up with such overwhelming inherited memories, to be dominated by narratives that preceded one's birth or one's consciousness, is to risk having one's own stories and experiences displaced, even evacuated, by those of a previous generation." I put the word *Last* on a whole year of music. I am spending my life answering a silence I did not live through. I cannot tell you from inside whether that is fidelity or displacement. In 2024, writing about what Holocaust memory has been made to license since October 7, a dispute I do not bring into this essay, she turned on the looser readings of her own word. Many have claimed that to inherit such a legacy is to suffer transgenerational trauma: "That is not how I have meant 'postmemory' to be understood." In the next line she adds, "But now I find myself rethinking earlier assumptions." And further on: "although it could have been me, importantly and decidedly, it was not me—it was their story and not ours. Our postmemories do not grant us the status of victims." I take that as a rule for this essay. It was not me. I was born six years after.

As near as I can say it, then, that is why I cry at Sinn Sisamouth. The voice reaches a room in me that was furnished before I was born. I am not mourning something I lost. I am recognizing something I was given — with the last verse missing. Whether I was handed that room or built it from what I was handed, I cannot tell from inside. Of the whole idea of inherited memory, Hirsch says: "If this sounds like a contradiction, it is, indeed, one, and I believe it is inherent to this phenomenon."

## 5. The Metteyya thing to do

For a long time I was shy of the thought that finally came, because it can be said so wrongly. My tradition holds that when the Dharma is wholly lost, Metteyya comes. In Cambodia, in one terrible local sense, that day arrived: the temples, the monks, the books. And I was born six years after the wheel stopped, on the very day my country marks its liberation — into the silence, shaped by the silence. It would be easy, and it would be false, to make that into a story about being someone special. So let me say it exactly, and then guard it.

I do not claim to be Metteyya. Nobody sane claims that, and my tradition — which I trust more than I trust my own enthusiasms — reserves such names for beings only it may recognize. What I claim is smaller and, I believe, truer, and it is the sentence at the head of this essay: *born into the aftermath of what was lost, and shaped by its silence, the Metteyya thing to do is fill it with song and loving-kindness again.* Metteyya means loving-kindness. Whatever else the prophecy is, it is a direction: after the silence, the world is remade in mettā. And here is the part that saves the thought from grandiosity, the part I hold hardest: **the archetype is universal.** Everyone is born into some aftermath. Every family has its interrupted songs; every person is shaped by silences they did not choose and cannot name. The capacity to answer silence with loving-kindness is not conferred on chosen ones — my tradition teaches that mettā is cultivable by anyone, protects all beings, excludes none. If the archetype lives anywhere, it lives in all of us, equally, the way the songs live in us: before memory, waiting to be played.

So this is not an essay about my destiny. It is an essay about an assignment that I believe is standing open in every life, and that in mine happens to wear Cambodian clothes.

One guard on that guard. What is universal here is the assignment, never the loss. Hirsch's field has learned to "relate, without conflating, distinct historical catastrophes," and saying that everyone has some inherited weather does not make anyone's weather the same as anyone else's. She also asks how we can "carry their stories forward without appropriating them, without unduly calling attention to ourselves, and without, in turn, having our own stories displaced by them." An essay that opens on its author's tears has already failed the second clause. I do not have a way out of that. I can only say it.

## 6. Filling the silence

Let me say concretely what answering the assignment looks like for me — briefly, because the work should speak in its own venues, and because the point of this essay is the shape, not the résumé.

The song, first, literally. Cambodia's Golden Age — the cinema of the sixties, the music that Sisamouth and Sothea poured into it — was not merely entertainment; it was a young nation hearing its own modern voice for the first time, and it was interrupted mid-phrase. The revival of that inheritance is already being carried by people I honor — the archivists who rescued the surviving films, the survivor-musicians who founded schools so the masters' repertoire could pass from the last bodies that held it into new ones. I am adding what I can add: stories built for the form that reaches children — the form that plants culture *before memory*, which is one way a song outlives the people who sang it. The first of these is a fairy tale about a queen who knows she will not live to raise her daughter, and who sings to her unborn child every day anyway; about a daughter who cries at a melody she has never heard; about a song reassembled, verse by verse, from the scattered people who each kept a piece. I did not construct that story to illustrate this essay. I dreamed it more than twenty years ago and have carried it since — and only this year did I understand that I had been dreaming about my own country, and my own inheritance, and the missing last verse in my own chest.

Here Hirsch would stop me, and I want her to. The central warning of her 2008 article is about the images a generation brings to a past it is hoping to find: they "may be screen memories—screens on which we project present or timeless needs and desires." And she asks: "What more potent such image than the image of the lost mother and the fantasy of her recovery?" My fairy tale is that image: a mother who will not live, a daughter who knows her song. That the dream is old does not answer her, because an old dream can still be a need. That I understood it only this year is exactly what understanding after the pattern looks like. In the introduction to her 2012 book, under the heading "Beginnings," she writes "We've learned to be suspicious of origin stories," and then tells hers anyway. So I keep the dream, and I stop offering it as evidence. She grants that much herself: the generation after needs such familiar forms, and they work like a shield, screens "that absorb the shock," that "paradoxically" "reinforce the living connection between past and present." A story for children can carry a song forward. It cannot prove how the song got into me.

The teaching, second. With my father, I am helping transcribe the Khmer Tipiṭaka — the books that burned — into forms that cannot burn: digital, distributed, and eventually etched into fused silica — 3B-Crystal™ — designed to outlast every institution that could ever again make a bonfire. Books were burned once because books could be burned. That is a solvable problem, and solving it is an act of loving-kindness toward every future generation that might otherwise be orphaned the way mine almost was.

And the conditions, third — the longest arc, and the one where I will simply gesture at the companion paper. The tradition that raised me has an old, precise idea about what righteous power is *for*: not to walk anyone's path for them — no one can — but to keep the world in the condition where paths can be walked. To feed what needs feeding, protect what needs protecting, ask the wise what is wholesome, and then — this is the clause I love most — to let go of the whole apparatus when it has done its work, the way a traveler leaves a raft at the far shore instead of carrying it on his head forever. Everything I am building is built to end that way: to restore conditions, and then to be laid down. The institutions are the cart, not the crossing. The crossing belongs to each of us alone.

## 7. The last verse

I said at the beginning that understanding my sadness changed what I am doing with my life. Here is the change, stated as plainly as I can.

I used to think healing meant the sadness would end. I no longer think that, and I no longer want it. A cadence that resolves does not erase the minor key that made the resolution mean something; it gives the ache somewhere to land. The melancholy I carry is the presence in me of everyone who sang before the interruption — it is, in the only sense I can defend, which is not a memory of theirs that anyone could hand over but what I have made from what they managed to carry, *their love, still arriving*. To wish it gone would be to wish them silent twice. What I wished for, without knowing it, all those years of writing songs called *Last* — was not silence. It was the last verse. The one that was being composed when the music stopped. The one that no archive holds, because it was never written down; it was sung into the children, before memory, where no fire reaches.

I think it is still there. I think it is in all of us who were born just after — Khmer or otherwise, because every people has its interruptions and every child its inherited weather. And I think the way it comes out is the way it went in: someone stands in a market, or a temple courtyard, or a kitchen, and plays the opening phrase with enough love, and a stranger's body answers before their mind can ask why — and then, past thought, past grasping, the ending rises: not remembered, *released*.

When the Dharma is lost, my tradition says, loving-kindness itself comes to restore the world. I was born into a small local silence six years deep, in a country that had lived the whole prophecy in miniature, and I have finally stopped asking why the sadness follows me. It isn't following me. It is *waiting* for me — to do the Metteyya thing, the thing any of us can do, the thing you can do with whatever silence you were born into.

Fill it with song. Fill it with loving-kindness. The interrupted song can finally resume — and it resumes in whoever is willing to sing the next phrase.
