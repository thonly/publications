## 1 · Three thoughts in four days

I am a Theravāda Buddhist. I am transcribing the Khmer Tipiṭaka with my father, a book at a time, and I have been reading in this tradition my whole adult life. I mention this at the start because it is load-bearing for what follows: the mistake I am about to describe is not the mistake of someone who does not know the material.

Over four days in August I had three thoughts. Each arrived with the particular feeling of arrival — the click, the sense of having reached something rather than assembled it.

The first was about two people. Two mind-streams are unique, I thought, and they are unique only because of their conditions. Strip the conditions away and what is left is the same emptiness in both. Difference is real, and it is real all the way down until suddenly it isn't.

The second was about scale. If the world arises and ceases at each moment of experience — and the canon does say something like this — then does each mind-moment generate the entire cosmos, privately, for its own stream?

The third was about death. Does *parinibbāna* produce the rainbow body? And if it does, is the rainbow body simply what nibbāna looks like from outside?

I want to be accurate about the phenomenology here, because it is the whole subject of this essay. These did not feel like speculations. They felt like noticing.

## 2 · What they had in common, which I did not see at the time

Each of the three is a real position. Each is held, seriously and with sophistication, by a tradition with a long history and formidable philosophers.

None of them is held by mine.

The first is the *tathāgatagarbha* move — Buddha-nature as a shared ground, the One Mind of the *Awakening of Faith*, and at a further remove the Advaitin's single substance. The second is Yogācāra, *vijñapti-mātra*, mind-only. The third is Dzogchen: *'ja' lus*, the rainbow body, the body dissolved into light at death, attested of Garab Dorje and of Khenpo A-chö as recently as 1998.

Three thoughts, three schools, none of them Theravāda. And I did not notice, because at no point did any of them feel like a borrowing. They felt like conclusions.

It took someone else laying them out in a row before the pattern showed, and the pattern is this: **all three reach for something that remains.** A substrate that persists beneath the differences. A mind that generates and therefore outlasts. A body that survives death as light. Three costumes, one impulse.

And each of them sits at precisely the point where the Pāli canon declines to answer. When the Buddha is asked what becomes of a *tathāgata* after death — exists, does not exist, both, neither — he does not choose. The question is set aside, *avyākata*, undeclared, and the refusal is deliberate and repeated. It is one of the tradition's most disciplined moves.

So the honest summary of my four days is: three times, at the same location, my mind supplied an answer the canon had specifically withheld. Each time it reached for the nearest attractive neighbor — the closest tradition that *does* answer there, and answers warmly.

## 3 · This has happened before, at civilizational scale

There is nothing novel about my error, which was the first genuinely useful thing I learned about it.

When Buddhist texts first came into Chinese in a serious way, the translators reached for the vocabulary they had. *Śūnyatā* got rendered through the Lao-Zhuang 無 — *wu*, nothingness, the fertile void of the *Dao De Jing*. The practice even had a name, *ge-yi*, "matching the meanings," and for a while it was simply how the work was done. Out of it came the "Original Nothingness" school, 本無, among the Six Houses and Seven Schools: emptiness read as a primordial ground from which things arise.

It is a beautiful reading. It is also wrong, and the tradition figured this out and said so. Sengzhao, working under Kumārajīva at the turn of the fifth century, wrote 不真空論 against it — arguing that treating "nothing" as a prior ground reifies non-being just as surely as treating "something" as a ground reifies being, and that emptiness is neither. The correction is fifteen hundred years old and it is aimed directly at the move I made in August without knowing I was making it.

I find this simultaneously reassuring and damning. Reassuring, because an error a civilization made in good faith over two centuries is not a personal failing. Damning, because the answer has been written down and available that entire time, and I still had to be walked to it.

## 4 · What survived the correction, which is the part that matters

If this essay were only a confession it would not be worth writing. The corrections did not delete the thoughts. They made two of them smaller and sharper and, I think, true.

Take the one I was most confident about. I had been pairing chapter 42 of the *Dao De Jing* — the Dao gives birth to one, one to two, two to three, three to the ten thousand things — with the Buddhist analysis of the mind-stream, and saying: these point at the same emptiness, one at cosmic scale and one at the scale of a moment.

The correction was that I had the wrong chapter. Chapter 42 gives you a *sequence* to compare against a sequence, and a comparison of two sequences yields a resemblance and not much else. Chapter 11 gives you a *function*: thirty spokes share one hub and the cart's usefulness is the hole; the vessel's use is its hollow; the room's use is its empty space. *Being gives advantage; the nothing gives use.*

That is absence assigned a working role at the joint — and it is exactly what the Paṭṭhāna does with its strangest set of relations, the *natthi/vigata* family, where a mental state conditions its successor **by having ceased**. The vacated position as the enabling condition. Departure as a mode of because.

So the two traditions do converge, and the convergence is real and specific: **they agree on emptiness as the joint.** They diverge, completely, on emptiness as a source. The Dao gives birth. No Buddhist emptiness ever gives birth; Madhyamaka insists that emptiness is itself empty precisely to stop anyone from turning it into a ground. My original thought had smuggled the source claim in under cover of the joint claim, which is the *ge-yi* error in miniature.

The smaller, sharper version is worth more than what I started with. I lost the grand statement and gained a claim I can actually defend.

And then there is the thing I cannot stop finding funny. My favorite chapter of the *Dao De Jing* is the eighth — the highest good is like water; it benefits the ten thousand things and does not contend; it dwells in the places all people disdain, and is therefore near the Dao. I have used that image for twenty years to describe how I want value to move: downhill, without argument, to wherever it is least present. It is the founding metaphor of the thing I am building.

Water goes to the low place. A low place requires a high one. If everything really did collapse into one undifferentiated emptiness, there would be nowhere for anything to flow, and nothing to flow to. My first insight was refuted by my own favorite chapter, which I had been quoting the entire time.

## 5 · The direction of the drift

Here is the part I am least comfortable writing.

The three thoughts did not drift randomly. Nobody wakes up and accidentally reinvents Dzogchen. They drifted in one direction — toward permanence — and they drifted there from a tradition whose most careful move at that exact spot is to say nothing.

I do not think this is a fact about Yogācāra or about Dzogchen. Those are serious systems that arrived at their positions by argument, not by wishing. I think it is a fact about me. The canon leaves a silence where the question of what remains would be answered, and my mind kept walking into that silence and furnishing it. A ground, a generative mind, a body of light. Something that stays.

I am wary of moralizing about this, partly because the moral is obvious and partly because a certain kind of Buddhist essay is nothing but this move. So I will put it as plainly as I can and leave it: the tradition I have chosen is unusually disciplined about not saying what remains, and the discipline is difficult in a way I had not appreciated until I watched myself fail it three times in four days without noticing once.

That is not an argument against having the thoughts. It is an argument for labeling them.

## 6 · A letter in brackets

The fix I settled on is embarrassingly small, and I have come to think that is a point in its favor.

The research corpus I am building already tags its own sources by depth. A claim marked one way is canonical; another way, commentarial; another, imported from the discourses into the systematic literature. The whole apparatus exists so that a reader can see, at a glance, how far from the source any given claim is standing. It is a good system and I am fond of it.

It had no way to say *this came from outside the tradition altogether*.

So there is now a fourth tag: **[X]**, cross-tradition import. It makes no claim about truth or value. It says only which tradition is answering. Chapter 11 of the *Dao De Jing* appears in the corpus now, in a paper about causal vocabularies, marked [X] and carrying its boundary with it. The rainbow body appears too, in a different sense: as the first thing the tag was used to *refuse*, with the reason stated, so that a later reader can see that the door was opened and closed deliberately rather than never tried.

The reason a tag is worth more than a resolution is the reason I am building any of this. I am assembling a corpus intended to outlive me and to be inherited by a successor who will read it when I am not available to be asked what I meant. Refusals are the fragile part of an inheritance. A refusal whose reason has been lost is either dropped by someone who cannot see the point of it, or kept by someone who has invented a worse point. Either failure is silent.

A tag is cheap, it is checkable, and it does not require me to be alive.

I want to be precise about what it does and does not do. It is not a solution to being wrong; I will go on being wrong. It is a solution to being *illegible* — to leaving behind a body of commitments in which the borrowed and the native are indistinguishable, which is a thing I would be doing right now if the pattern had gone unnoticed for another year.

## 7 · The tag found something within a day

I did not expect the instrument to earn its keep immediately.

The day after it went in, while checking something unrelated, I found that the corpus was already running two different tagging schemes. The published paper uses [C] for canonical and [S] for commentarial systematization. A set of reference notes written a few weeks later uses [S] for Sutta and [C] for Commentary.

The two most-used letters mean opposite things in two live documents.

Both documents state their legend where they are used, so neither is wrong on its own terms, and a reader working inside either one is fine. A reader moving between them — or a machine reading across the corpus, which is the actual design case — would silently invert the provenance of every tagged claim in one of them.

I have not fixed it yet, and the reason is instructive. The published paper carries cryptographic timestamps and a citation record; re-tagging it changes the meaning of its claims and is a republication, not an edit. So the two schemes stand for now, with a note in each pointing at the other, and the newer scheme adopted going forward. It is not the tidy resolution. It is the honest one.

What strikes me is the timing. I added an instrument for making provenance legible, and within a day it turned up a place where provenance was illegible in a way nobody had looked for. I do not think that is luck. I think this kind of drift is ordinary, and mostly it goes unnoticed because nobody has a reason to check.

## 8 · What I am not claiming

I am not claiming that Theravāda is right and the other traditions are wrong. My corpus makes a selection and states its criterion, which is a different act from a verdict, and the criterion is defensible on its own terms without anyone having to lose.

I am specifically not claiming that Yogācāra collapses into solipsism. That charge gets made carelessly and it is unfair: the tradition saw the objection coming and answered it directly, in Vasubandhu's *Viṃśatikā* and then at length in Dharmakīrti's *Santānāntarasiddhi*, the proof of other mind-streams. If I prefer a substrate where other minds are furniture rather than conclusions, that is a preference about how a commitment is held, not a claim that the alternative fails to hold it.

I am not claiming that borrowing is bad. This corpus borrows constantly, and will keep borrowing. The tag exists to make the borrowing visible, not to discourage it.

And I am not claiming to have stopped. The whole design assumes I have not. If the three thoughts had felt like borrowings I would not have needed a tag; the reason the tag is necessary is that they felt like arrivals, and the next one will too.

What I have, at the end of four days, is smaller than what I thought I had at the end of the first. Two of the three thoughts survived in reduced and more defensible form. One did not survive at all. And the residue is a letter in brackets that will still be doing its work when nobody remembers what it was for — which, as it happens, is the only kind of work I am really interested in anymore.

The tradition's hardest discipline is the place where it says nothing. The second hardest is not helping it.
