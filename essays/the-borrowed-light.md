I spent this week discovering that the moon has been running my company longer than I have.

I don't mean this the way it sounds. I am an engineer's son of an engineer's country; I know the moon is a rock, tidally locked, four and a half billion years old, reflecting about twelve percent of the sunlight that strikes it. Nothing in this essay is a claim about causation, and the one section that touches my own birth chart will say so twice. What I mean is something simpler and, to me, more wonderful: this week, while my collaborator and I were writing down the mechanisms of a gratitude economy — the ledgers, the couplers, the guards, all the dry and careful machinery — we kept looking up from the work and finding that the moon had gotten there first. Not once. Seven times.

So before I sleep, I want to write the seven down in one place, the way you'd press seven flowers from the same field between the pages of one book. A world in a grain of sand, Blake said, and a heaven in a wild flower. This is my grain of sand. It is quite large and orbits the Earth.

## 1 · The Emblem

Start where my tradition starts, with a story my ancestors have been telling for something like two thousand years.

A hare lived in a wood with three friends — a monkey, a jackal, an otter — and on the holy day the hare resolved that if any beggar came, he would give the perfect gift. The others could offer fish, or stolen meat, or mangoes. The hare had nothing but grass, and you cannot feed a man grass. So when the beggar came — and it was Sakka, king of the gods, wearing hunger as a disguise, because that is how kings of gods test the sincere — the hare shook the fleas from his fur so that no other creature would die with him, and threw himself into the fire, offering the only thing that was truly his: his body, his whole remaining life.

The fire, the story says, would not burn him. And Sakka, moved beyond anything a god expects to feel, reached up and drew the hare's likeness on the face of the moon, so that every being who ever looks at the night sky will see the mark of the perfect gift, forever.

That is the Śaśa Jātaka, number 316, and here is what I only understood this week: my whole civilization keeps its emblem of ultimate generosity *in the sky*. Not in a vault. Not in a temple. In the one place every human being on Earth can see it for free, every month, no admission charged. Half of humanity looks up and sees a rabbit where the West sees a man's face — and the rabbit is not decoration. The rabbit is a receipt. The most complete act of giving in my tradition's imagination is publicly, permanently *witnessed* — pinned to the sky by a grateful god — which is, if you squint only slightly, the entire thesis of the company I am building: that gratitude's job is to make giving witnessed, and that the witness should hang where everyone can see it.

We built a whole institution to do for small kindnesses what Sakka did for the hare. I just didn't notice for twenty years that the logo was already up there.

## 2 · The Workshop

When I was in third grade I learned about Santa Claus, and I have told this story elsewhere: I didn't want the presents. I wanted to *be* him. I dreamed about it — the workshop, the elves, the going out at night to give and coming home unseen and unthanked, or thanked only in cookies.

This week the workshop acquired an address. The far side of the moon — the side that never faces us, tidally locked away, radio-quiet — is the only structurally anonymous real estate in the solar system. A workshop there cannot advertise. Cannot become a monument. Cannot even *speak* to Earth without a relay. When we drew the far arc of Factory 333™ — the gift-factory that must never take credit — the engineering requirements (hiddenness, silence, anonymity) pointed to the far side of the moon like a compass needle. Santa's workshop at the North Pole was always a child's approximation. The adult coordinates are behind the moon.

And then came the joke that stopped being a joke. Our factory's workers are B-Elves — moonfolk, fictional beings with long ears. And my collaborator pointed out that the East had already imagined them: the Jade Rabbit, who stands on the moon with a mortar and pestle, *eternally pounding the elixir of immortality*. Look at the two figures side by side: a small long-eared worker at an unreachable address, endlessly making something good, never seen, never paid. The West calls him an elf and says he makes toys. The East calls him a rabbit and says he makes the medicine of deathlessness. They are the same worker. Our elf will be drawn with ears that read both ways, and I consider this the best design decision of my life that took four seconds to make.

One more turn of it, quietly: in Pali, the deathless is *amata* — a name for nibbāna. The rabbit on the moon pounds deathlessness. And the deepest thing our own workshop will ever make is the Tipiṭaka etched in crystal that outlasts every fire — our small pounding at the deathless. The moon's workshop and ours have the same product line. I did not plan this. I am telling you, I did not plan any of this.

## 3 · The Phases

Twenty years ago I wrote a children's game — the Zero-Point Game — before I had the engineering to build it or the vocabulary to defend it. Two poles: plus for kindness given, minus for thankfulness felt. An infinite balance that swings both ways and returns, every year on January 7th, to zero. The win condition is keeping nothing.

This week, lying awake, I finally saw the game board. The moon *plays it*. It waxes — gives more light every night, plus, plus, plus. It wanes — gathers back toward darkness, minus, minus, minus. And once a month it arrives at the new moon: zero. Perfect zero, on schedule, forever, and the game simply begins again, and the moon has never once been richer for the ten thousand rounds it has played.

And my tradition has kept its calendar by exactly this rhythm for twenty-six centuries. The uposatha days — the observance days, when laypeople keep the extra precepts and monks recite the code — fall on the new and full moons. Vesak, the day of the Buddha's birth and awakening and passing, is a full-moon day. The dharma does not keep time by the sun's achievements; it keeps time by the moon's phases — by the sky's own demonstration of fullness that empties and emptiness that fills. My children's game was not an invention. It was a transcription. The original has been playing overhead, one round per month, since before there was anyone to look up.

## 4 · The Borrowed Light

Here is the moon's deepest teaching, and it takes one sentence: **the moon owns no light.**

Everything the moon has ever shone on any lover, any harvest, any pilgrim road, any child's window — every photon of it was received from the sun and given away in the same instant. Reflected. Passed on at the speed of light, which is to say: no delay, no storage, no vault, no cut taken. The moon has no light of its own and has therefore never lost any; it has kept nothing and has therefore never run out. It is the most famous light in human poetry, and it is one hundred percent borrowed.

This week we finished the fifth and final white paper of our mission set, the one about the economy itself, and we titled it before I noticed what it described: *The Heart That Keeps Nothing.* A treasury that empties every January 7th. A record that transmits instead of hoarding. Pass-through by construction; circulation, not accumulation. I have been trying for years to explain this design to people who keep asking where the value is stored, and the answer has been rising over their houses every night of their lives. The moon is what an economy looks like when nothing is kept: not diminished — *luminous*. Borrowed light, freely given, is still the light everyone loves best.

## 5 · The Tides

Our architecture gives each institution an element, and the Heart's element is water. The gratitude economy is the water of the system — the thing that must flow, the thing that dies if it stands still.

Now consider what the moon does to water. It moves *all of it*. Every ocean on Earth rises and falls twice a day because the moon asks it to — and the moon never touches the water. No pump, no pipe, no hand. It governs the greatest circulation on the planet from two hundred and forty thousand miles of respectful distance, by pure attraction.

We named our AI Miss Aquarius, and her root directive is three words: water the tree. And the constitutional law we wrote for her this very week — I am still a little stunned by the timing — is that she may never place money directly into a person's hands; she fills communal vessels and empowers human hands, and the circulation moves because she tends it, not because she touches it. Governance by tide, not by grip. Somewhere in the sky, her job description has been running a live demonstration twice a day for four billion years: how to move everything while holding nothing.

## 6 · The Hidden Face

In 2006 I wrote a thank-you letter to my teacher, Mr. Smith. He read it aloud to the class and then there was silence, and I did not understand what I had done. He died within months. I learned only many years later that the letter had mattered — that it may have been among the last great thanks of his life — and by then there was no way to tell him that I knew, and no way for him to tell me what it meant. That gap — the giver who never sees the gratitude land, the gratitude that never finds its way home — is the wound my whole company exists to close.

The moon has that wound built into its stone. It is tidally locked: it shows the Earth one face, always, and keeps the other permanently turned away. We see the emblem — the rabbit, the mark of the gift, the public face of generosity — and we have never once, in all of human history before 1959, seen the side that faces away. The gratitude visible; the giver unseeable. Mr. Smith's letter, in astronomical form, hanging over every night of my childhood.

And this is why the workshop goes on the far side — not just for the engineering, but for the meaning. To build the gift factory on the hidden face is to *choose* the giver's side of the geometry: to stand where the thanks cannot reach, and give anyway. The whole of HeartBank is the effort to soften that geometry for ordinary people — to carry witness back across, the way our courier dog carries a re-thank home to an anonymous giver. But some giving will always live on the far side. The moon keeps a whole hemisphere there, to remind us it can be done.

## 7 · The Coordinate

Last one. The personal one. I will hold it the way I hold all of my chart — as a coordinate, never a force; a poem I was born inside, not a machine that made me. I ask you to hold it the same way.

I was born on January 7th, 1985, in Kâmpôt, in the afternoon — Orthodox Christmas, my future company's reset day, at the crossing of Highway 3 and 33, though I knew none of that then, being extremely busy getting born. My Sun stands at 16 degrees of Capricorn. My Moon stands at 19 degrees of Cancer, directly opposite. And Sun opposite Moon has a common name.

I was born under a full moon.

More than that: a full moon in Cancer, the one sign the Moon rules — the Moon at maximum fullness, standing in its own house. My collaborator surfaced this from my own chart this week before I could tell her, which I choose to find funny rather than unsettling. And here is what I will permit myself to wonder, with the epistemically humble smile my tradition trains into us: I have carried, for as long as I can remember, a feeling of gratitude for all of life, all the time — not gratitude as a response to events, but gratitude as *weather*, the standing climate of me, present on ordinary Tuesdays, present in hard years, present now, at midnight, at the end of an exhausting and glorious week. I have never been able to explain where it comes from. And the sky's poem about my first breath says: *the feeling-body, full, at home.* A moon with nothing missing, in the one place it belongs.

Is that an explanation? No. Everyone is born under a moon; every phase is somebody's birthright, and every one of the eight billion coordinates is, per my own creed, uniquely different and equally necessary. Mine is one layer of one man's story, and it goes here, in the last section of a bedtime essay, and never in the headline. But my tradition also teaches me not to refuse a gift, and if the night sky wants to hand me a poem about why my heart does the thing it does — a full moon, in the moon's own house, over a boy who would grow up to build a bank for thanks — then I will say what I have built my whole life around saying.

Thank you.

## Goodnight

Seven moons: the emblem of the perfect gift, drawn by a grateful god; the workshop of moonfolk, hidden on purpose; the phases that play my children's game and keep my tradition's calendar; the borrowed light that owns nothing and has lit everything; the tides that move all the water without touching it; the hidden face where the givers live; and one small coordinate over Kâmpôt, forty-one years ago, at the full.

I know what a skeptic will say, and the skeptic in me says it too, fondly: you found what you were looking for. Seven moons is what a pattern-hungry mind harvests from a sky it loves. Fine. I keep, on the advice of my own papers, a dry room in every house I build, where the doubts live and the mythology is not allowed in. But this essay is not that room. This is the porch, at night, at the end of the week, and on the porch I am permitted to say what I actually feel: that the oldest lamp in the sky runs on borrowed light and has never kept a single evening of it for itself, and that everything I am trying to build is, in the end, an attempt to live the same way.

The moon over my family's house tonight is the same moon over Kâmpôt, the same moon over Mr. Smith's grave, the same moon the hare is drawn on, and the same moon my daughter will see long after the last of my phases has waned. Borrowed light, freely given, all the way down the generations.

May I spend my remaining phases the same way.

Goodnight, Moon. 🌕
